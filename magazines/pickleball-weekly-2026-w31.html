# THE PICKLEBALL GAZETTE **Issue 31 — Week of August 4, 2026** *The fastest-growing sport in America* --- ## 1. COVER STORY ### The Dink Wars: How Three Players Are Rewriting the Rules of Soft Game Dominance When Ben Johns walked off the court after his 7-5, 7-4 victory over TJ Washington at the PPDA Nationals in April, the crowd didn't cheer for the winner — they cheered for the *point*. Not a single ace, not a smashing overhead. The final three points were nothing but surgical dinks, wristy resets, and a drop shot so delicately placed it died at the kitchen line. Someone in the stands yelled, "That's not pickleball, that's chess with paddles." They were right. This week, that observation has become a rallying cry. Three players — Johns, Seattle's rising star Maria Jose Romero, and 42-year-old veteran Dirk Kujawinski — are being credited with reinventing the dink game, and the debate over whether the sport is becoming too passive is heating up faster than any heated rally. Romero, who turned pro in 2024 after dominating the collegiate scene at USC, has been making waves with her "wave dink" — a softer, higher-arcing version of the traditional dink that forces opponents out of position without giving them a clean attack. In her quarterfinal match this past weekend, she hit 47 consecutive dinks without unforced error, drawing 22 faults from her opponent before closing out the set. "I used to think the dink was just a setup shot," Romero told *The Gazette* from her training base in Scottsdale. "Now I see it as the entire point. The ball doesn't need to go fast. It needs to go *where they don't want it*." Kujawinski, playing in his fourth Masters Tour event this season, has taken a different approach. Known for his "pop-and-dink" strategy — hitting a soft pop from the baseline that draws the opponent up, then sliding into the kitchen for a dominating dink exchange — he's gone 11-0 this season in the 40+ division and recently broke the record for most consecutive dink-rally points won in a single game. The controversy, however, lies in the rules. Several players have petitioned the USA Pickleball rules committee to consider a "passivity warning" standard similar to tennis' foot-fault rule, arguing that matches have become excessively slow. PPDA President Craig Spiegler pushed back in a press release Monday: "The dink is the heartbeat of this sport. If fans want more power, we encourage more power. But we will not regulate a shot because it's 'boring' to some." Johns, when asked about the debate, smiled and said simply: "Winning is never boring." --- ## 2. PLAYER SPOTLIGHT ### Alex Currie: From Barista to Billie Jean King Cup Contender At 23 years old, Alex Currie is the unlikely face of pickleball's next generation. A former barista in Portland, Oregon, Currie discovered the sport at a community center open gym in 2021 and spent the next three years playing exclusively for fun — until a viral video of his 12-10 comeback in a local tournament caught the attention of coach and former pro John Zeilstra. "This kid had something I'd never seen," Zeilstra said. "Not just talent, but *patience*. He could stand at the kitchen line and dink for twenty minutes without ever rushing. It's a personality trait, not just a skill." Currie's rise has been meteoric. Ranked outside the top 500 in early 2024, he cracked the top 50 by mid-year and is currently sitting at No. 28 in the men's singles rankings — the youngest player in the top 30. He claimed his first Masters Tour title in June at the Chicago Open, dropping just one set en route to the trophy. What sets Currie apart isn't just his game; it's his approach. He spends two hours every morning on footwork and positioning before he even picks up a paddle. "I watch a lot of tennis," he explained. "Rafa Nadal used to say that 70% of points are won or lost before the ball is even struck. I take that seriously." Currie also runs a free weekly clinic at the Portland Pickleball Project, coaching kids from underrepresented communities. "This sport opened my life up," he said. "The least I can do is open the door for someone else." When asked about his goals, Currie didn't hesitate: "I want to be the first American to win a mixed doubles Grand Slam. That's it." --- ## 3. GAME ANALYSIS ### The Match That Redefined Doubles: Johns & Cruz vs. Washington & Selena **Event:** PPDA U.S. Open Semifinals, August 2, 2026 **Score:** Ben Johns/Tiffany Cruz def. TJ Washington/Selena Stanford, 8-6, 6-7 (4-7), 10-7 (supertiebreak) If you only watch one pickleball match this month, make it this one. What unfolded at the Phoenix Convention Center was a masterclass in contrasting philosophies — and a masterclass in clutch execution. The first set was a dink war to remember. Johns and Cruz, known for their aggressive third-shot drop game, kept the ball low and deep, forcing Washington and Stanford into uncomfortable retrievals. The set went to 8-6 after 34 minutes of almost uninterrupted kitchen play. Johns hit 19 third-shot drops, 14 of which landed in the "kill zone" — the sweet spot between the kitchen line and the center line. The second set belonged to Washington and Stanford. The 34-year-old Washington, playing arguably the best pickleball of his career, attacked the net with ferocity. His signature "scoop return" — a soft, looped return that drifts high over the net and drops just inside the kitchen — proved impossible for Cruz to handle. Stanford, anchoring the backcourt with precision, hit 11 winners off the return of serve. The set went to the tiebreak, which Washington and Stanford won 7-4. But the supertiebreak? That was pure drama. With the score at 8-8, Johns called a timeout — an unusual move in a supertiebreak. He gathered Cruz and said something the cameras caught on a nearby player's headset: "Trust the dink. They're tired." It was prophetic. Johns and Cruz won five straight points — three dinks, a kill shot, and a net cord lucky bounce that dropped straight down. The crowd erupted. Stanford, visibly frustrated, hit the next serve into the net. 10-8, Johns/Cruz advance. **Key Stat:** Johns hit 31 successful dinks in the supertiebreak — the highest single-set total in PPDA history for a supertiebreak. --- ## 4. TIPS & TRAINING ### Five Drills to Master Your Kitchen Presence Whether you're a USAP 3.5 or a 5.0, the kitchen is where matches are won. Here are five drills our coaching staff recommends this week: **1. The Wall of Dinks** Stand at the kitchen line with a partner. Alternate dinking back and forth for 60 seconds, aiming for the center of the court. Every time you hit a ball outside the center two feet, you start over. This builds consistency and patience. *Time: 5 minutes.* **2. Third-Shot Drop Progression** Partner at the kitchen line, you at the baseline. Hit third-shot drops until you land 10 in a row. Start with 20 feet behind the baseline, then move back to 25 feet, then 30. This builds the touch and arc needed for high-level play. *Time: 10 minutes.* **3. The "No-Error" Rally** Play a full game with your partner where the goal is zero unforced errors. If you hit it out or into the net, you restart the game from 0-0. This isn't about winning — it's about staying patient and making smart shot selections. Most players last 3-5 games before blowing it. *Time: 15 minutes.* **4. Poach and Recover** One player at the net, one at the baseline. The net player practices stepping in to poach on any ball that crosses the center line, then recovering to the correct side. The baseline player feeds soft dinks and lobs. Rotate after 5 minutes per side. *Time: 10 minutes.* **5. The Mental Reset Drill** After every point — win or lose — physically turn your back to the court, take one deep breath, and turn back around. This builds a habit of mental reset between points, which is critical at the competitive level. *Time: Built into every game.* **Coach's Note:** "The players I see improve the most are the ones who practice *patience*, not just power. If you can dink for 10 minutes without rushing, you can win any match." — Coach Lisa Park, USA Pickleball Level 3 Certified --- ## 5. COMMUNITY CORNER ### Grassroots Pickleball: The Stories Behind the Sport **Portland's "Pickleball for All" Initiative Hits 1,000 Participants** The Portland Pickleball Project celebrated a major milestone this week: 1,000 unique participants in its free community program, which has been running since 2023. The program offers free equipment, coaching, and court time to residents of all ages and skill levels. "We started with 12 people showing up on a Tuesday night," said founder Marcus Chen. "Now we have a waiting list. This sport is a lifeline for our community." **Florida Community Center Builds Sixth Court This Year** The Seminole County Community Center in Florida has broken ground on a six-court outdoor complex, funded entirely by local donations and a state grant. The center's director, Rita Moreno, said the existing four courts are "packed from 6 a.m. to 10 p.m. every day." The new facility will open in spring 2027. **High School Pickleball: North Carolina Leads the Nation** According to the National Federation of State High School Associations, North Carolina now has 47 schools with official pickleball programs — the most in the country. Texas follows with 38, and California with 31. The growth is driven largely by after-school clubs, with several states now recognizing pickleball as an official interscholastic sport. **New Park in Austin Named After Local Legend** The City of Austin announced this week that it will rename the Barton Springs Pickleball Complex in honor of Dorothy "Dot" Harlan, a 78-year-old who has played competitively for over 15 years and founded the city's first senior pickleball league in 2010. Harlan was visibly emotional at the announcement: "I never thought I'd see my name on a building. This is for every older person who was told they were 'too old' to compete." --- ## 7. HISTORY LESSON ### How Pickleball Found Its Name — And Why It Stuck It's one of the most charming origin stories in all of sports. In the summer of 1965, on Bainbridge Island, Washington, three fathers — Joel Pritchard, Bill Bell, and Barney McCallum — were looking for a way to entertain their families during a lazy July afternoon. Badminton equipment was handy, but the badminton court was in disrepair. So they improvised: they lowered the net, used a wiffle ball, and crafted paddles from plywood. The game was an instant hit. Friends came over. Kids loved it. And then someone brought a dog. "Pickles," Pritchard's daughter Susan would later recall, "came running to see what was going on." According to the most widely accepted version of the story, the game was named after Pickles, the family's black Scottish terrier, who would chase the ball and bring it back — much like a pickleball player retrieves a shot. "It reminded us of the rowboat race called 'pickle boats,' where the crew is made up of rowers from other boats," Pritchard reportedly said. "This was the same idea — a mixed bag of different elements." Some controversy exists, however. Joan Pritchard, Joel's wife, claimed in a 2001 interview that the name came from the "pickle boat" analogy alone, and that Pickles the dog was just a happy coincidence. Either way, the name stuck. The sport spread slowly at first — from Bainbridge Island to the Pacific Northwest, then to Arizona and Florida in the 1970s, fueled by retirees who discovered the game's accessibility and social nature. By the 1990s, the USA Paddleball Association (later USA Pickleball) had been founded, and standardization of rules began. The explosion in popularity in the 2020s has been unprecedented. According to the Sports & Fitness Industry Association, pickleball is now the fastest-growing sport in America, with over 4.8 million players in the U.S. alone — up from just 1.3 million in 2019. From a backyard game on Bainbridge Island to a multi-billion-dollar industry, pickleball's journey is far from over. As the old saying goes: "It started as a summer pastime. It became a way of life." --- *Thanks for reading Issue 31 of 2026. See you on the courts.* **— The Team at The Pickleball Gazette**
