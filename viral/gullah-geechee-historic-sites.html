The Essential Gullah Geechee Historic Sites You Should Visit

Updated August 2026 · Gullah Geechee Biz · Heritage & Travel

The Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor stretches from the Cape Fear River in North Carolina down through South Carolina to St. Augustine, Florida. Within it lie the landmarks where African American history, language, and culture are preserved and taught. Here are the sites that matter most.

1. Penn Center — St. Helena Island, SC

A National Historic Landmark and one of the most important African American heritage sites in America. Founded in 1862 as a school for freedpeople, it later hosted Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. Home to archives, a museum, and the historic York W. Bailey Museum.

2. Caw Caw Interpretive Center — Ravenel, SC

On land formerly worked by enslaved Africans, this Charleston-area nature preserve interprets the history of rice cultivation and the enslaved Gullah people who made the Lowcountry rice economy possible.

3. Sweetgrass Cultural Arts Pavilion — Mount Pleasant, SC

A dedicated space honoring the sweetgrass basket weavers and the 300-year-old African art form that continues to thrive along the Carolina coast.

4. Daufuskie Island — SC

A remote Sea Island where Gullah language, culture, and community life remained remarkably intact. Its historic schools, praise houses, and cemeteries preserve a way of life now increasingly rare.

5. Fort Sumter and Fort Moultrie National Park — Charleston

Where the Civil War began — and where the free Black soldiers who helped preserve the Union and liberate enslaved people are remembered in the nation's military history.

6. Sapelo Island & Fort Frederica — Coastal Georgia

Georgia's Sapelo Island is home to the historic Hogg Hummock community, and nearby Fort Frederica preserves the story of colonial settlement alongside the African-descended people who built and worked the Lowcountry frontier.

7. St. Augustine, Florida — Southern Anchor of the Corridor

The Fort Mose Historic State Park marks the first legally sanctioned free Black settlement in the present-day United States, anchoring the southern end of the Gullah Geechee corridor.
