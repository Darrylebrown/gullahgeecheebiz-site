The Gullah Language Is Still Spoken — Here's Why It Matters

Published August 09, 2026 · Gullah Geechee Biz

The Gullah language is an English-based creole built on West African foundations, drawing on languages like Mende, Twi, and Yoruba. It grew on the Sea Islands and Lowcountry, where our ancestors — isolated on the rice and Sea Island cotton plantations — forged a language of their own that let generations speak to one another, and speak freely.

For a long time, outsiders dismissed it. Then, in the 1940s, the scholar Lorenzo Dow Turner documented just how African the language truly is. His landmark 1949 work, Africanisms in the Gullah Dialect, proved that hundreds of our words echo the West African languages our ancestors carried across the Middle Passage — a discovery that rewrote how the world understood our speech.

The number of fluent speakers today is small, and our elders — the people who carry the language — are precious. But this is not a story of a language dying in silence. It is a story of a language fighting back.

Educators, linguists, and community organizers are teaching Gullah in classrooms and workshops. Children are learning words their grandparents grew up speaking. Translators are bringing our proverbs and our music into new ears. The work is quiet, patient, and ongoing — and it matters because a language is not just vocabulary; it is a way of seeing the world.

When we speak Gullah, we speak the voice of the rice fields, the praise houses, and the homes where our grandmothers told stories. To lose the language would be to lose a part of who we are. We are choosing otherwise.
