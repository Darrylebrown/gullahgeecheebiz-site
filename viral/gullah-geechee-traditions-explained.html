10 Essential Gullah Geechee Cultural Traditions, Explained

Updated August 2026 · Gullah Geechee Biz · Culture & Heritage

The Gullah Geechee people of the South Carolina and Georgia Sea Islands carry a living African heritage across the Atlantic. These ten traditions are the heartbeat of that culture — preserved across more than 400 years.

1. The Gullah Language — An English-Based Creole

The Gullah language blends English with West and Central African grammar and vocabulary. Phrases like "I binya" (I have been here) and "duh" (to be doing) descend directly from African roots. It is one of the most studied Creole languages in the world.

2. Sweetgrass Basket Weaving

Coiled sweetgrass baskets woven by hand trace their technique directly to West African basket making. The art, centered on Mount Pleasant and St. Helena Island, is recognized by the Smithsonian and passed down through families.

3. The Ring Shout

A counterclockwise communal dance with hand-clapping and call-and-response singing — one of the oldest surviving African American performance traditions, preserved on the Sea Islands.

4. Gullah Cuisine and One-Pot Cooking

Red rice, okra soup, Frogmore stew, she-crab soup, and benne wafers all descend from African ingredients and cooking techniques — rice cultivation and okra stewing being the most direct.

5. Praise Houses and Hush Harbors

Small island churches, called praise houses, held worship, singing, and testimony. Hush harbors were secret meeting places where enslaved people practiced African-rooted spirituality beyond the eyes of overseers.

6. Rootwork and Folk Medicine

Gullah "root doctors" and grandmothers preserved herbal healing traditions using sassafras, elder, and other Lowcountry plants — a living African medicinal legacy.

7. Storytelling and Gullah Jack

Oral tales, trickster stories, and remembered African fables keep history alive. Figures like Gullah Jack, a root worker in the Denmark Vesey revolt, anchor these narratives in real struggle.

8. Graveyard and Funeral Custom

Gullah burial customs — shell-decorated graves, bottle trees, and ceremonies that send the spirit home across the water — reflect deep West African beliefs about death and ancestry.

9. Call-and-Response Music

From spirituals and praise songs to the "shout," African call-and-response patterns passed directly into the Sea Islands' musical tradition and helped give birth to American gospel, blues, and jazz.

10. Land Stewardship and Heirs' Property

Decades of passing farmland down across generations — without formal wills — created "heirs' property." This tradition, alongside modern preservation efforts, keeps Gullah Geechee families anchored to the land.
