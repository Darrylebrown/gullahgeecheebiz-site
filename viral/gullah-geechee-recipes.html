Gullah Geechee Recipes: The West African Roots of Lowcountry Food

Updated August 2026 · Gullah Geechee Biz · Food & Heritage

Every pot of red rice and every bowl of okra soup carries a direct line back to West Africa. The Gullah Geechee people — descendants of enslaved Africans on the South Carolina and Georgia Sea Islands — built American Southern cooking on rice, okra, and one-pot ingenuity. Here are the essential dishes and the history behind them.

1. Gullah Red Rice — The Signature Dish

Red rice is the crown jewel of Lowcountry Gullah cooking. Long-grain rice is slowly simmered with tomato, smoked pork or sausage, onion, and bell pepper until the grains soak up deep, savory color and flavor. It echoes the rice pilaus of West Africa — a direct culinary inheritance from the region that made colonial South Carolina the "Rice Kingdom."

2. Okra Soup (Okra "Stew" or Gumbo)

Okra came to the Americas from West Africa with the enslaved — its very name derives from the Igbo word okuru. Gullah cooks simmer okra with tomatoes, shrimp, and crab to make a rich thickening stew that predates and parallels Louisiana gumbo. It is one of the clearest living African food traditions in North America.

3. Frogmore Stew

Also called Lowcountry boil, this one-pot feast of shrimp, smoked sausage, corn, and potatoes — seasoned with Old Bay-style spice — is tailgating and family-gathering fare that traces its roots to the Gullah Geechee shore communities of St. Helena Island and the surrounding Sea Islands.

4. Benne Wafers

Benne (sesame) seeds reached the Lowcountry from West Africa, where the word bene (or benni) is used for the sesame plant. Gullah cooks turned these nutty seeds into crispy benne wafers — a beloved Charleston and Sea Island snack with a true African surname.

5. Shrimp and Grits

Stone-ground Lowcountry grits topped with sautéed shrimp in a savory butter sauce is now a Southern classic. Its heart is Gullah: the grits and the Lowcountry seafood both come from the very land and waters the Gullah Geechee people have stewarded for centuries.

6. Hoppin' John and One-Pot Traditions

Black-eyed peas cooked with rice — Hoppin' John — is a New Year's tradition believed to bring luck. Like red rice and okra soup, it is one-pot West African-style cooking: economical, hearty, and built on the ingredients of the rice coast.
