The Definitive Guide to Sea Islands Lowcountry History

Updated August 2026 · Gullah Geechee Biz · History & Heritage

Stretching from Charleston, South Carolina, to northern Florida, the Sea Islands hold one of the most consequential and least-told stories in American history. This guide traces the Lowcountry from the era of rice and slavery to the Gullah Geechee preservation movement of today.

The Geography of the Sea Islands

The barrier islands of the Lowcountry — St. Helena, Edisto, Wadmalaw, Johns, Lady's Island, Daufuskie, Sapelo, Ossabaw, and Hilton Head — were shaped by rice agriculture and relative isolation. That isolation preserved a unique African-derived culture that scholars now call Gullah Geechee.

The Era of Rice and Enslavement

Beginning in the 1700s, enslaved people from the "Rice Coast" of West Africa (Senegambia, Sierra Leone) brought deep knowledge of tidal rice cultivation that made South Carolina enormously wealthy. Their language, agriculture, and spirituality formed the foundation of Gullah culture.

The Port Royal Experiment (1862)

When the U.S. Navy captured Port Royal Sound in 1861, thousands of enslaved Sea Islanders fled to Union lines. The Port Royal Experiment became one of the first large-scale attempts to educate, employ, and compensate formerly enslaved people — a bold rehearsal for Reconstruction.

Penn School and Penn Center (1862)

Founded on St. Helena Island during the Civil War, Penn School became a model for Black education and later a meeting place for civil rights leaders. Today the Penn Center National Historic Landmark District preserves this legacy — one of the cornerstones of Gullah Geechee history.

The Combahee River Raid (1863)

Harriet Tubman, guided by Sea Island scouts, led the Combahee River Raid, freeing more than 700 enslaved people in a single night and dealing a devastating blow to the plantations. It was the first major military operation in U.S. history planned and led by a woman.

Reconstruction and the U.S. Colored Troops

Thousands of Gullah Geechee men served in the U.S. Colored Troops from the Sea Islands. Their military records today are some of the richest genealogical sources for Lowcountry families, documenting names, families, and communities.

The 20th Century and the Struggle to Hold the Land

Through the Great Migration, Jim Crow, and rapid resort development, Gullah Geechee families fought to hold the land passed down for generations. Heirs' property disputes and the loss of island access became defining struggles of the modern era.

The Gullah Geechee Preservation Movement

In 2006, Congress created the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor to protect and promote this living culture from North Carolina to Florida. Today, researchers, educators, and cultural institutions are working to ensure the story is never lost again.
