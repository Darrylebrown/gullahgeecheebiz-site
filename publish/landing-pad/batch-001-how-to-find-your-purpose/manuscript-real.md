# How to Find Your Purpose

## A Gullah Geechee Guide to Living with Direction

### By Darryl Elliott Brown
### Gullah Geechee Biz

---

## Introduction: The Question That Brought You Here

Every one of us has stood where you are standing now — at the edge of a question so large it feels like weather: *What am I supposed to do with my life?* You have come to this book because the usual answers have not satisfied you. The job titles. The checklists. The ten-step plans that tell you to "find your passion" as though passion were a lost set of keys.

This book takes a different road. It comes out of the Gullah Geechee tradition of the Sea Islands of South Carolina and Georgia — a culture that has survived for more than four hundred years by knowing something important about purpose: **purpose is not discovered in isolation, and it is not a destination. Purpose is a way of walking.** It is what your people called *the way*, the road your ancestors walked, and it is still under your feet.

The Gullah Geechee people kept African traditions alive on the Sea Islands longer than almost any other community in America — the language, the stories, the rice and indigo skills, the spirituals, the praise houses, the sweetgrass baskets, the deep belief that a person belongs to something larger than themselves. And in that belonging, they found what so many of us search for: a sense of being exactly where you are supposed to be, doing exactly what you are supposed to do.

This is not a book about quitting your job on Monday. It is not about finding one perfect passion and chasing it. It is a book about **reconnecting to the deep currents of your own life** — the skills you were given, the people you were given to, the place you were given — and letting those currents carry you into work and life that feel like you.

In the chapters ahead, you will:

- Learn the **four anchors of purpose** drawn from Gullah Geechee wisdom — *heritage, gift, place, and people*.
- Do the real work of **listening to your own story** for the clues you have been overlooking.
- Separate **true purpose from borrowed ambition** — the difference between what the world told you to want and what you actually want.
- Build a **daily practice** that keeps you pointed in the right direction even on ordinary Tuesdays.
- And finally, take your first **real steps** — not a grand overhaul, but the honest, doable beginning that every meaningful life is built from.

There is an old saying in the Sea Islands: *"If you want to go fast, go alone. If you want to go far, go together."* Finding your purpose is a going-far journey. So we will go it together — you, this book, and the long line of people behind you who are quietly hoping you find your way.

Let us begin where every Gullah Geechee story begins: with remembering.

## Chapter 1: Remembering — Why Your Past Is the Road Map

The Gullah Geechee people have a practice that may seem strange to the modern ear: they tell the same stories over and over. The same tales of Brer Rabbit and the briar patch. The same accounts of how Grandmother crossed the water. The same songs, sung the same way, in the same season, year after year.

To a modern mind, this can look like repetition. To the Gullah Geechee mind, it is something else entirely: **memory is a compass.** The stories are not being repeated because people forgot them. They are being repeated because the people know that a story carries more than facts — it carries direction. Every time a story is told, it reminds the young ones who they are, whose they are, and what is expected of them.

### The Purpose in Your Own Story

Here is the first truth of this book: **your purpose is already written in your past.** Not as fate — you are free to choose — but as a set of clues. The Gullah Geechee see a person's life as a woven basket: every strand of experience, every hardship, every gift, every person who shaped you is part of the pattern. You cannot see the whole basket while you are living inside it, but the strands are all there.

Think for a moment about your own strands:

- **The hardships you survived.** What did they teach you that other people need to know?
- **The moments you lost track of time.** What were you doing? Who were you helping?
- **The problems that keep finding you.** What do people consistently bring to you?
- **The praise that embarrassed you.** What did people thank you for that felt like no big deal to you — but was a big deal to them?

These four questions are not casual. In the Gullah Geechee tradition, when a young person was trying to find their way, the elders did not hand them a personality test. They asked the young person to *remember* — to tell the story of their own life and listen for what the story was saying. The answer to "What am I supposed to do?" was almost always hiding inside the story they had already lived.

### The Strands That Were Not Your Choice

Here is the part that is harder: some of the most important strands in your basket were not chosen by you. Your family. Your place. The struggles of your people. The gifts you were born with and the ones you had to fight for.

The Gullah Geechee do not treat these unchosen strands as limitations. They treat them as **the material you were given to build with.** You do not get to choose your raw materials — nobody does. But you do get to choose what you build.

If you grew up around illness, you may know what the sick and their families need. If you grew up poor, you know things about dignity and money that the wealthy will never learn. If you grew up in a family of teachers, or farmers, or storytellers, you carry a craft in your blood that the world still needs. The question is never "Was this strand my choice?" The question is "What can I build with this strand that helps somebody?"

### A Practice: Telling Your Own Story

Before you read another page, do this. Take out a piece of paper — or open a note on your phone — and write, in five sentences or fifty, the story of your life so far. Not the résumé version. The real version. The version you would tell a wise elder who asked, "Child, how did you get here?"

Write about:
1. Where you come from and who raised you.
2. The hardest thing you have ever been through.
3. The moment you felt most alive.
4. The people you love and what they need.
5. What you would do with your life if money were not a factor — and no one would judge you.

Do not edit. Do not make it sound impressive. Just tell the truth.

When you finish, read it back to yourself slowly. Circle the sentences that make your chest tight. Those circled sentences are not accidents. They are your story trying to tell you where your purpose lives.

**Purpose begins with memory.** You cannot know where you are going until you have honored where you have been. In the next chapter, we will look at the second anchor: the gifts you carry — the ones you have been dismissing because they come too easily.

## Chapter 2: The Gift You Keep Dismissing

There is a story told in the Sea Islands about a man who complained to the Lord that he had no talents. "I can't sing like my sister," he said. "I can't preach like the deacon. I can't farm like my uncle. I am a man with no gift."

And the Lord said, "Child, the gift I gave you is the one you use without thinking. You are the one who sits with the grieving. When somebody's heart is broken, they do not call the singer. They do not call the preacher. They call *you*. That is your gift, and you have been using it all your life without once giving Me thanks for it."

The man went home and looked at his life with new eyes — and saw that it was true. He had been doing his purpose for forty years, thinking it was nothing, because it came too naturally to count.

### The Curse of the Easy Gift

Here is one of the strangest truths about purpose: **the gifts that point to your purpose are usually the ones you dismiss.** Why? Because anything that comes naturally to you feels like it must be easy for everyone. You assume that because *you* can do it without strain, it cannot be valuable.

But think about it from the other side. The things that are easy for you are very often the things that are hardest for the people around you. If organizing comes naturally to you, look at how many people around you are drowning in chaos. If you can explain things simply, look at how many people are confused. If you can stay calm in a crisis, look at how many people panic.

The Gullah Geechee have a word for this kind of knowing — it lives in the body, not the head. When you are using your real gift, you do not feel exhausted; you feel fed. Time moves differently. You forget to check your phone. The work itself is the reward, and you would do it even if nobody paid you — which is exactly why it is the thing you should build your life around.

### What to Look For

Your gift is probably hiding in one of these places:

**1. What people ask you for.** Listen to the requests that come your way. "Can you look at this?" "Can you fix this?" "Can you talk to my cousin?" "Can you write this down?" People do not ask you for things they think you are bad at. The requests that keep coming are a map of your gift.

**2. What you do when nobody is watching.** What do you do on a free Saturday? What do you research for fun? What videos do you watch — not to escape, but because they feed something in you? Your unpaid attention is one of the most honest signals you have.

**3. What you cannot stand to see done badly.** Irritation is a clue. The person who cannot stand to see a plant dying is a gardener. The person who cannot stand to see a sentence written badly is an editor. The person who cannot stand to see a child bullied is a protector. What makes you angry reveals what you love — and what you love reveals what you were made for.

**4. What you did as a child.** Before the world told you what was practical, what did you love? The child who lined up her dolls and taught them is a teacher. The child who took apart the radio is an engineer. The child who organized the neighborhood games is a leader. Your childhood self was not naive — your childhood self was *accurate*. The world just talked you out of it.

### A Practice: The Five-Person Survey

Ask five people who know you well — a family member, a friend, a coworker, a neighbor, someone you have helped — this exact question:

*"When you think of me, what is the thing I do that helps people the most? What would you miss if I stopped doing it?"*

Write down their answers, even the ones that embarrass you. Especially the ones that embarrass you. You are looking for the pattern that appears in all five answers. That pattern is your gift — the one you have been dismissing because it comes too easily.

Then ask yourself the question that turns a gift into a purpose:

*"Who in the world needs this gift right now — and how could I get it to them?"*

**Your gift is not what you are best at. It is what you give away that makes you feel most yourself.** In the next chapter, we will look at where that gift is supposed to be used — the anchor of place, and why your corner of the world matters more than you think.

## Chapter 3: Place — Why Your Corner of the World Matters

There is a Gullah Geechee expression you will still hear in the Lowcountry: *"I'm from here."* It sounds simple, but it is not. When an elder says "I'm from here," they are not giving you a mailing address. They are telling you about belonging — about a specific patch of land where their people have lived, worked, worshipped, and been buried for generations. They are telling you that they have roots, and roots are how a person knows which way is up.

Modern life has taught us to be suspicious of place. We are told to go where the opportunities are, to keep our options open, to be willing to relocate at a moment's notice. And there is freedom in that. But there is also a cost: a person without a place is a person without a compass. When everywhere is home, nowhere is home, and it is very hard to find your purpose when you are not sure where you belong.

### The Purpose of Place

Your place is not just geography. Your place is:

- **The people right around you** who have needs you can see with your own eyes.
- **The problems of your community** that you understand from the inside, not from a report.
- **The history that shaped you** — the specific story of your family, your town, your people — that gives your work meaning.
- **The networks you already have** — the cousin, the deacon, the old teacher, the neighbor — that no outsider can replicate.

The Gullah Geechee did not preserve their culture by dreaming of somewhere else. They preserved it by pouring their lives into the place they were — by building praise houses and schools, by working the same rice fields their ancestors worked, by telling the stories on the same soil where the stories happened. They understood something that the modern world has largely forgotten: **you do not need to go somewhere else to find your purpose. You need to look at where you are with new eyes.**

### The Danger of the "Elsewhere" Trap

Here is a test. When you think about your purpose, does your mind immediately jump to somewhere else? "I could really make a difference if I were in a big city." "If I had that degree, then I could..." "Once I move, everything will change."

The elsewhere trap is seductive because it lets you postpone the work. As long as your purpose lives in a hypothetical future place, you never have to start it here, today, with what you have. But here is the truth the elders would tell you: **if you cannot find purpose in the life you have, you will not find it in the life you are planning.** The move, the degree, the promotion — those things change your address, not your direction.

That is not to say you should never move or never change. Some purposes genuinely require a new place. But before you decide that, you owe it to yourself to look hard at the place you are already in. Look at your own street, your own congregation, your own workplace, your own family. What breaks there? What is missing there? What could you do, starting this week, with the resources you already have?

### The Place Audit

Walk through your corner of the world this week with new eyes. Notice:

1. **The needs.** What are people around you struggling with? What do you hear complained about at church, at the shop, at the bus stop, at the dinner table?
2. **The gaps.** What used to exist in your community that is gone now — the shop, the program, the person who helped everyone? What did they provide?
3. **The gifts already there.** Who around you is already doing good work? How could you join them, learn from them, or help them grow?
4. **Your unique angle.** Of all the people in the world, you are the only one with your exact history, your exact skills, your exact relationships in this exact place. What does that combination equip you to do that no one else can?

### The Story of the Well

There is a story from the old rice plantations, still told among the people: a man spent years dreaming of building a great well in a far city where the water was sweet. He told everyone about it. He drew plans. And all the while, in his own yard, his family was carrying water from a neighbor's well a mile away, because the well at his own house had gone dry and he had never thought to dig it again.

Do not be that man. Look at the ground under your own feet first. The water you are looking for may be closer than you think — in the place you already are, among the people who already know you, solving the problems you already understand.

**Purpose is not found by escaping your place. It is found by loving your place enough to serve it.** In the next chapter, we turn to the fourth anchor — the people, and the strange truth that your purpose is usually about somebody else.

## Chapter 4: The People — Your Purpose Is Usually About Somebody Else

Ask most people what they want from life, and they will answer in the first person: I want to be successful. I want to be happy. I want to feel fulfilled. I want to matter.

The Gullah Geechee would answer differently. The question was never "What do I want?" The question was "**Who am I for?**" And that single shift in the question changes everything.

### The Paradox of Purpose

Here is the paradox at the heart of this book: **the more directly you chase your own fulfillment, the more it eludes you. And the more you pour yourself into the lives of others, the more fulfillment finds you.**

This is not a platitude. It is a psychological fact that has been confirmed again and again: people who live for a cause larger than themselves report more meaning, more resilience, and more satisfaction than people who optimize only for their own pleasure. The Gullah Geechee knew this centuries before the researchers confirmed it. That is why their culture was built on *mutual aid* — the "each one, teach one" tradition, the sick committees, the praise houses where everyone had a role, the understanding that when one person fell, the whole community bent down to lift them.

Your purpose is hiding in the answer to this question: **whose life are you trying to make better?**

### Finding Your People

Not everyone is your people. One of the most freeing discoveries you can make is that you are not called to help everyone — you are called to help *someone*. Trying to be everything to everyone is a fast road to burnout and a slow road to nowhere.

Ask yourself:

- **Who do you understand from the inside?** Not from sympathy, but from experience. If you have been through addiction, you understand the addict in a way the counselor's textbook never will. If you have been poor, you understand the poor. If you have raised a difficult child, you understand that parent. Your wounds are not just your wounds — they are your credentials.
- **Who keeps showing up in your life?** The same types of people keep appearing for a reason. The friend who always brings you her problems. The coworker who always asks your advice. The young person who always finds you. Notice who you attract — they are telling you who you are for.
- **Who can you reach that others cannot?** There are people in your life — in your family, your neighborhood, your church, your workplace — who will listen to you and not to anyone else. You have access that no expert has. That access is a responsibility and an opportunity.

### The Question That Changes Everything

Here is the practice for this chapter. Sit quietly and ask yourself this question, and do not let yourself off the hook until you have an honest answer:

*"If I could change one thing about the world, and I had to do it for a specific group of people I know and love, who would those people be, and what would I change for them?"*

Write the answer down. Be specific. Not "help people" — *which* people? Not "make a difference" — *what* difference, for *whom*?

The Gullah Geechee have a saying: *"Every tub sits on its own bottom."* Every person must eventually stand on their own. But before they can stand, someone must show them how. Someone must be the elder who says, "Child, you can do this." Someone must be the one who believes in them before they believe in themselves.

Why not you?

**Your purpose is the intersection of your gift and the world's need — and the world's need is always, at the bottom of it, a specific person's need.** In the next chapter, we will talk about the hardest part of all: separating what you actually want from what you were told to want.

## Chapter 5: Borrowed Ambition — Separating Your Purpose from the World's

By now you have done real work: you have told your story, named your gift, looked at your place, and asked who you are for. But there is one more enemy you must face before you can move forward — and it is the sneakiest one of all.

It is the voice of **borrowed ambition.**

Borrowed ambition is the set of goals you absorbed without choosing them. The career your parents dreamed of for you. The lifestyle your friends post about. The definition of success your culture handed you: the house, the title, the follower count, the retirement date. None of it is evil. All of it is borrowed.

The Gullah Geechee have a story that speaks to this. A young man came to his grandmother and said, "Grandmother, I want to be somebody." And she said, "Child, you already are somebody. What you want is to be *somebody else* — and that is a full-time job with no pay."

### How Borrowed Ambition Disguises Itself

Borrowed ambition never announces itself. It wears the costume of your own voice. Here is how to catch it:

**1. It comes with "should."** "I should go to law school." "I should take this promotion." "I should start posting more." The word *should* is the signature of borrowed ambition. Your real purpose rarely says "should" — it says "I can't not." It pulls you; it does not nag you.

**2. It is about the finish line, not the running.** Borrowed goals are all about the outcome — the title, the money, the recognition — and strangely empty when you imagine actually doing the work. Your real purpose, by contrast, makes the work itself feel meaningful. Imagine the day-to-day reality of the goal. If the daily reality feels dead, the goal is borrowed, no matter how shiny the finish line looks.

**3. It is defended by fear.** When you imagine letting go of a borrowed goal, you feel panic — not grief, but panic. What will people think? What will I be without it? That panic is the tell. A real purpose, when you imagine losing it, gives you grief. A borrowed one gives you fear of judgment. Grief means it was yours. Fear of judgment means it was rented.

**4. It belongs to someone you can name.** This is the most practical test of all. Can you name whose ambition it is? "This is my father's dream for me." "This is what everyone in my high school was supposed to become." "This is what my wife's family expects." If you can name the owner, it is borrowed — and it is time to decide whether you want to buy it or give it back.

### The Two Questions

To separate what is yours from what you have borrowed, ask yourself two questions about each goal you are carrying:

*Question one: If no one I know were watching — no one to impress, no one to prove wrong, no one to make proud — would I still want this?*

*Question two: If I knew I could not fail, and also that no one would ever congratulate me, would I still choose this?*

If the answer to both is yes, it is yours. If the answer to either is no, you are probably carrying someone else's load.

### The Fear of Starting Over

Here is what usually happens when a person faces the truth about borrowed ambition: they do not deny it — they just decide it is too late to change. "I'm too old." "I've come too far." "I have too many obligations." "This is what I trained for."

The Gullah Geechee would answer that with a story from the sea. A fisherman's boat was damaged in a storm, and for years he kept patching it, sailing it, cursing it, because it was the boat he had. It leaked. It turned slowly. It embarrassed him. And then one day he realized: the boat was not his purpose. *Feeding his family was his purpose.* The boat was just one way to do it — and it was a bad way. He sold the boat, bought a net and a cart, and started hauling fish to the market by land. He fed his family better than ever.

It is never too late to change your boat. It is only too late if you keep telling yourself it is.

**Your purpose cannot be rented. It must be owned — chosen by you, for your own reasons, paid for with your own life.** When you have separated what is yours from what you were handed, you are finally ready to walk. In the next chapter, we will build the daily practice that keeps you walking.

## Chapter 6: The Daily Walk — Practices That Keep You Pointed True

Purpose is not a decision you make once. It is a direction you keep choosing — and direction is kept by practice, not by intention. The Gullah Geechee understood this in their bones. They did not merely *believe* in their culture; they *practiced* it daily — the language spoken at home, the food cooked on Sunday, the stories told at night, the songs sung in the field and the praise house. Practice is how a people keeps its way for four hundred years. Practice is how a person keeps their purpose for a lifetime.

You do not need to overhaul your life tomorrow. You need small, faithful practices that point you back to your purpose every single day.

### The Daily Practices

**1. The Morning Question.**
When you wake, before you check your phone, ask yourself: *"Who am I serving today, and how will I serve them?"* You do not need an elaborate answer. One sentence is enough: "Today I will listen to my son." "Today I will do honest work for my customers." "Today I will write the page I promised myself." This question, asked daily, is a compass check. It takes thirty seconds and it changes the direction of your whole day.

**2. The Daily Gift.**
Every day, use your gift for someone — even in a small way. The writer writes one true paragraph. The fixer fixes one thing. The comforter makes one call. The teacher explains one thing to one person. Your gift is like a well: if you only draw from it when you are desperate, it goes stale. Draw from it daily, even in small amounts, and it stays sweet — and it keeps telling you who you are.

**3. The Weekly Review.**
Once a week — the Gullah Geechee would say on Sunday evening, after the praise house — sit with your week and ask three questions:
- Where did I feel most alive this week? What was I doing?
- Where did I feel most drained? What was I doing?
- Did I move toward my purpose or away from it — and what will I do differently next week?

Do not judge yourself. Just observe. The week is a teacher; the review is how you take its lesson.

**4. The Monthly Saying.**
The Gullah Geechee have a tradition of proverbs — short, wise sayings that carry the whole culture in a sentence. Once a month, write down one sentence that describes your purpose *right now*. Not forever — right now. "I am learning to help families cook healthy food on a budget." "I am building the confidence to teach my class." "I am healing enough to help others heal." Saying your purpose out loud, in writing, once a month, keeps it real. When it changes, let it change. The saying is a snapshot, not a contract.

### What to Do on the Hard Days

There will be days when the practices feel pointless. Days when your purpose seems like a foolish dream and the borrowed ambitions look wiser. Here is what to do on those days:

**Remember your people.** The Gullah Geechee never walked alone. When a person faltered, the community carried them — literally, in the old days, when a sick neighbor needed food or a grieving family needed the fields tended. On your hard days, do not isolate. Go to your people. Tell them the truth. Let them carry you for a while. And be ready to carry them when their hard day comes.

**Remember your story.** Go back to the story you wrote in Chapter 1. Read the circled sentences. You wrote them in a moment of honesty, and they do not lie — even on the days when you want them to.

**Do one small thing.** Purpose on a hard day is not a grand gesture. It is one small act of service, done anyway. Make the call. Write the paragraph. Fix the thing. Small acts done on hard days are how purpose becomes character.

### The Rhythm of the Tide

The Gullah Geechee live by the tide. The tide comes in; the tide goes out. Nobody curses the outgoing tide — it is part of the rhythm, and the same water that leaves will return. Your motivation will have tides too. There will be seasons of full energy and seasons of low water. Both are natural. Neither means your purpose has failed.

What matters is not the tide. What matters is that you keep the boat pointed true, so that when the water rises — and it always rises — you are ready to move.

**Purpose is kept by small daily practices, not by one great resolution.** Now you are ready for the final step: not planning your purpose, but starting it.

## Chapter 7: The First Step — Beginning Before You Are Ready

Everything in this book so far has been preparation. You have remembered your story. You have named your gift. You have looked at your place. You have found your people. You have separated your purpose from borrowed ambition. You have built your practices.

Now comes the moment that separates the people who find their purpose from the people who only read about it: **you must start — before you are ready.**

The Gullah Geechee have a saying about this: *"You can't learn to swim in the church house."* At some point you have to get in the water. And here is the secret the water teaches you: you were never going to be ready by waiting. Readiness is not a condition you reach; it is a feeling you earn by acting. The first step always feels premature. That is not a sign you are wrong. That is a sign you are finally doing it.

### The First Step Is Smaller Than You Think

Most people never start because they are aiming at the whole mountain. "I'm going to write a book." "I'm going to start a business." "I'm going to become a counselor." Those are not first steps — those are summits. You cannot take a summit as a step.

Your first step is smaller than you think. It is:

- Writing one page, not a book.
- Making one call, not building the network.
- Taking one online course, not earning the degree.
- Offering to help one person, not launching the nonprofit.
- Showing up once, not building the habit.

The Gullah Geechee built their famous sweetgrass baskets one coil at a time. You cannot rush a sweetgrass basket — the grass must be gathered, dried, and bound, coil upon coil, and the basket takes shape only through patient repetition. Nobody looks at a finished sweetgrass basket and says, "How did they do that so fast?" They say, "How did they do that so patiently?"

Your purpose will be built the same way. One coil. One day. One small, faithful step.

### The First Week

Here is your assignment — not for someday, but for this week:

**Day 1:** Write one page toward your purpose. One page of the book. One page of the plan. One page of the curriculum. One page.
**Day 2:** Tell one person. Not for permission — for accountability. Say, "I'm doing this. Will you check on me?" The Gullah Geechee knew that a promise spoken to the community is a promise kept.
**Day 3:** Do the smallest possible version of the real thing. If your purpose is teaching, teach one person one thing. If it is writing, publish one post. If it is building, build one small piece.
**Day 4:** Serve one person with your gift — free, expecting nothing. Not as a loss leader; as a confirmation. Your gift, given, will tell you whether this is your road.
**Day 5:** Learn from someone who is already doing it. Ask them one good question. Most people will happily tell you what they wish someone had told them.
**Day 6:** Do the work again, and notice how you feel. This is the data that matters.
**Day 7:** Rest. Review. Write your monthly saying: *"I am becoming ______."* Fill in the blank with the truth of this week.

### What Starting Will Cost You

Be honest about this: starting will cost you something. It will cost you the comfortable story of the person who *could have* done it — the dream that never fails because it never gets tested. It will cost you some evenings and some weekends. It will cost you the risk of doing it imperfectly, in public, where people can see.

But consider what not starting costs: another year of the same question. Another year of borrowed ambition. Another year of feeling that you have a gift you are not using. That cost is higher — and it compounds.

The Gullah Geechee ancestors crossed an ocean they did not choose, and built a life on land that was not offered. They did not wait to be ready. They started with what they had — a language, a memory, a song, a skill, each other — and they built a culture that has outlasted every attempt to erase it. If they could start with that, you can start with what you have. You have more than you think.

### A Closing Word from the Elders

If an elder were sitting with you now, here is what they would say:

*"Child, you have been looking for your purpose like it is a lost thing. But a lost thing is only lost to the one who is not looking at it right. Your purpose was never lost. It has been walking with you all along — in the story you carry, the gift you give, the place you stand, the people you love. You have been living inside your purpose for years without calling it by its name. Now you know its name. So walk it. Not perfectly. Not all at once. But walk it — one coil, one day, one step at a time. And when you stumble, and you will stumble, do not lie in the road. Get up, remember who you are, and keep walking. The road is long, but you are not alone on it. You never were."*

## Afterword: A Note on the Gullah Geechee Tradition

The wisdom in this book draws on the living traditions of the Gullah Geechee people — the descendants of enslaved West and Central Africans who preserved their language, culture, and spirituality on the Sea Islands and coastal Lowcountry of South Carolina, Georgia, and North Florida. For over four hundred years, this community has practiced a way of life rooted in memory, mutual aid, faith, craft, and deep connection to land and water.

This book is not an academic history. It is a practical guide that takes its shape from that tradition's understanding of purpose: that a person finds direction not by escaping their history but by drawing strength from it; not by pursuing isolation but by serving a people; not by one grand decision but by a daily walk.

The proverbs, stories, and practices here are offered in the spirit of the tradition — which has always shared its wisdom generously — with respect for the community that kept them alive through the hardest centuries. Where specific stories are told, they are told in the manner of the tradition: to carry a truth, not to record an event.

May they carry you to your purpose.
