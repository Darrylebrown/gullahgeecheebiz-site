

# Gullah Geechee Stories: The Cunning Crab and the Rice Field

## Story #8

---

## Introduction

In de lowcountry, where de rice swamps stretch out like green carpets under a wide Carolina sky, de people of Gullah Geechee land have always lived close to de water and close to each odder. For hundreds of years, our ancestors — enslaved Africans who were taken from many different regions of de continent — kept their stories alive through de spoken word. dey told 'em on plantations, in shotgun houses, by candlelight during long nighttime watches, and on porch steps during summer evenings when de hummid air felt like a warm blanket.

Dis collection comes from dat tradition — from de Sea Islands dat stretch from northern Florida all de way up to southeastern South Carolina. Gullah Geechee is more dan just a language; it is a way of seeing de world, a way of honoring de ancestors, and a way of laughing at life even when it hard.

Story #8 in dis series comes from de Beaufort County area, where rice was once king and every field had to be watched day and night. It was passed down through many generations, first from grandmother to mother, den from mother to child, and now I pass it to you.

So come near, sit down comfortable, and listen well.

---

## The Story

Once upon a time, in a small rice farming community near de marshlands of South Carolina, dere lived a young man named Jonah. Jonah was clever — real clever — but he had one problem: he didn't like to work. He would wake up late, stretch himself out in de shade of de cypress trees, and watch de herons stand still in de water like they had all de time in de world.

"Yo' gonna get eaten by de mosquitoes," said his ma, Mrs. Thompson, one morning as she walked past him wid a basket full of sweet potatoes.

"I'm conservin' my energy, Mama," Jonah said, not movin' an inch. "A wise man knows when to rest."

"A wise man also knows when to work," she shot back, but she didn't argue further. She just shook her head and walked off toward de kitchen.

Now, dis community had a big rice field dat belonged to de whole town. It was a shared field — everyone contributed, everyone harvested, and everyone shared in de bounty. But it needed constant tending. De irrigation ditches had to be cleared, de bunds had to be repaired, and de birds had to be shooed away from de ripening grain.

Dis year, de rice was lookin' mighty fine. Tall, green, and heavy-headed, swayin' in de coastal breeze like it already knowin' it was gonna have a good harvest. But somewhere in dat field, a crab had made himself a home.

Now, dis wasn't any ordinary crab. Dis was a fiddler crab wid one oversized claw, and he was sly as sin. His name was Mr. Clarence, and he had learned real quick dat de rice field was full of little insects, worms, and fallen grains he could eat without liftin' a finger. He made himself a burrow near de main irrigation ditch, right where de water came in, and he settled in comfortable.

But Mr. Clarence had a bigger ambition. He wanted de whole field to himself.

One evening, as de sun dipped below de horizon and painted de sky in shades of orange and purple, Mr. Clarence scuttled to de edge of de field where Jonah sat leanin' against a fence post.

"Hey, long legs," Mr. Clarence called out in his crackly crab voice. "You got a minute?"

Jonah looked down, surprised. "You talkin' to me?"

"I sure am," said de crab, raisin' his big claw in a wave. "Name's Clarence. I got a proposition for you."

Jonah's ears perked up. He didn't see many crabs in his life, let alone crabs dat could talk and make propositions. He sat up straight. "A proposition? What kind of proposition?"

Mr. Clarence tapped his smaller claw against de dirt thoughtfully. "Well, you see, dis rice field? I've been watchin' it. It's fine rice. Real fine. But somebody gotta watch it, right? Gotta make sure de birds don't get too greedy, dat de alligators don't come wadin' through, dat nobody tampers wid de water flow. And I — well, I got concerns, Mr. Jonah. Real concerns."

Jonah studied de crab. "You got concerns?"

"Oh yes," said Mr. Clarence, and he let out a long, dramatic sigh. "See, last night I was patrol — well, I was takin' a little stroll along de western bund — and I see somethin' terrible. Somethin' ugly. Somethin' dat could ruin dis entire harvest."

"Whaddaya mean?" Jonah asked, now fully intrigued.

"De birds," Mr. Clarence said, his voice drop pin low. "Not just any birds, Mr. Jonah. A whole flock of 'em — cormorants, egrets, maybe even a few ospreys. They been scoutin' dis field. I saw 'em. I hear 'em conversin' up in de cypress trees. They're plannin' to move in come harvest time. And if dey move in, well… let's just say dis rice won't last two days."

Jonah's face fell. "Two days? But de harvest ain't for another three weeks!"

"Three weeks is all de time dey need to get comfortable," Mr. Clarence said grimly. "Trust me, I know birds. I got a cousin dat was devoured by a hawk when he was just a pup — well, a juvenile, I should say, but you get my meaning."

Jonah scratched his head. "So what am I suppose to do? I can't exactly chase 'em all away by myself."

"Now you're talkin' sense," said Mr. Clarence, and his big claw did a little happy tap. "See, I got a system. I know dis field better dan anybody — better dan de frogs, better dan de turtles, better dan de alligators for dat matter. I can tell you exactly where de birds will come from, when dey'll arrive, and how to scare 'em off before dey even think 'bout settlin' down. But I need somebody to help me. Somebody tall, somebody wide, somebody wid… strong legs, if you catch my meaning."

Jonah thought about it. He thought about how he didn't have to do any real work — just stand here and point, maybe wave a stick, maybe shout a little. "I could do dat," he said slowly. "But what do you give me in return?"

Mr. Clarence's eyes lit up like two tiny lanterns. "Well, I got access to de sweet spots in dis field — places where de best bugs live, where de grain falls thickest, where de water is deepest and coolest. Any place you want to go, I can show you. And more dan dat, I can keep you informed on every move dem birds make. We team up, we both win."

Jonah extended his hand. "You got yourself a deal, Mr. Clarence."

"Shake on it," said de crab, extendin' his big claw.

Jonah shook it. It was hard and clicky, but it felt real enough.

And so began de partnership.

Every morning, Mr. Clarence would scuttle to de edge of de field and call out to Jonah. "West bund! West bund got movement! Two egrets, maybe three, circlein' low — they scoutin' for a place to roost!"

Jonah would grab his stick and holler and wave it like a madman, chasin' de birds toward de open marsh where dey wouldn't come back. Den Mr. Clarence would say, "South ditch! South ditch lookin' clogged! If we don't clear it, de water back up and flood de young shoots!" And Jonah would wade into de muck and clear de ditch, even though he didn't really need to — de water was flowin' just fine.

Day after day, dis went on. Jonah worked just enough to keep Mr. Clarence happy, and Mr. Clarence fed him stories about bird movements and water levels dat were mostly made up anyway. Meanwhile, while Jonah was busy chasin' imaginary flocks and clearing imaginary ditches, Mr. Clarence was eatin' his way through de best patches of rice, burrowin' deeper and deeper into de heart of de field, gorging himself on de fruits of everybody's labor.

Weeks passed. De rice grew golden and heavy. De community began talkin' about harvest time.

One evening, as Jonah sat restin' on de bund, Mr. Clarence scuttled up lookin' more nervous dan ever.

"Jonah," he whispered, "I got bad news. Big news. The biggest news a crab could ever deliver to a man."

"What is it now?" Jonah asked, already tired.

"De birds came back," Mr. Clarence said, his voice tremblin'. "But not just a few. A whole army. Hundreds of 'em. They camped in de cypress grove last night. I heard 'em — I tell you, Jonah, I heard 'em plannin' dey attack. Tomorrow at dawn, dey comin' for dis field. All of 'em. And if dey get in here…"

He didn't even need to finish. Jonah's face went pale. "What do we do?"

"Now listen to me real close," Mr. Clarence said, and he pressed his face close to Jonah's ear. "I got a plan. It's risky, but it's our only chance. You gotta go to de center of de field, right to de oldest rice stalk — de one dat's been here longest, de one wid de blackened stem. You gotta dig around it. Just a little. You'll find a root, a big one, dat connects to everythin' else in dis field. It's de main root, Jonah. De mother root. If you pull it — just gently, mind you, not hard, just a firm tug — de whole field will shift. De birds won't be able to land. De water will drain out sudden-like, and dey'll be nothin' left for 'em but to fly elsewhere. It's de only way."

Jonah stared at him. "Dig up de whole field? For de birds?"

"Not de whole field!" Mr. Clarence said quickly. "Just a little hole. Just enough to find de root. Trust me, I've been studyin' dis ground. I know what I'm talkin' about."

Jonah didn't wanna believe him, but he was tired. Tired of worryin', tired of runnin' around, tired of fightin' birds dat he barely saw. He just wanted de problem to go away. So he walked into de field, found de oldest rice stalk he could see, and started diggin' with his bare hands.

He dug and he dug, pushin' aside clumps of earth and tangled roots, until — sure enough — he found somethin'. A thick, woody root, wider dan his wrist, runnin' deep into de mud. It felt connected to everythin'. Every stalk, every stem, every blade of rice in dis whole field seemed to pulse with life through dat one root.

"Now pull it, Jonah!" Mr. Clarence hissed from de edge of de hole. "Pull it now before it's too late!"

Jonah gripped de root and gave it a tug.

It didn't budge.

He tugged harder.

Still nothin'.

"Harder!" Mr. Clarence urged. "Pull with all your might!"

Jonah put his back into it. He planted his feet, gripped de root with both hands, and heaved.

*CRACK.*

De root snapped.

And everythin' changed.

De rice stalks around him began to droop, slow at first, den faster. De water in de ditches — de water dat had been carefully managed for months — began drainin' away into de mud. De whole field seemed to shudder, like a giant breathin' out for de last time. De birds, when dey came at dawn, found nothin' but soggy mud and breakin' stems. Dey circled once, confused, den flew off toward de open marsh where de water was still high and de fish were still swimmin'.

But de rice… de rice was ruined.

By de time de rest of de community came to inspect de field, Jonah was sittin' in de mud, his hands raw and bleedin', watchin' as Mr. Clarence scuttled quietly into a nearby burrow, full and content.

"What happened?" asked de elder, Mr. Delgado, a man who had farmed dis field since he was a boy.

Jonah looked down at his hands. He looked at de ruined stalks. He looked at de crab hole, which was now sealed shut.

"I tried to save it," he said quietly. "A crab — he told me de birds were comin'. He told me to pull de mother root. Said it would save de field."

Mr. Delgado's eyes narrowed. He walked over to de sealed crab hole and knelt down. He pressed his ear against de dirt. "A fiddler crab, by de looks of it," he muttered. "Clever critter. Too clever."

"What do we do now?" Jonah asked, his voice breakin'.

Mr. Delgado stood up and placed a heavy hand on Jonah's shoulder. "Now you learn, son. Now you learn dat de earth don't owe you nothin'. De water don't owe you nothin'. And de creatures in dis land? Most of 'em owe you nothin' neither. Mr. Clarence didn't care about dis field. He never did. He only cared about himself — eatin' de grain, drinkin' de water, livin' comfortable while you did de work. He used your laziness against you, just like a spider uses a fly's weakness."

Jonah looked at de crab hole one more time. "He tricked me."

"Every trickster tricks," Mr. Delgado said. "De question is, what you gonna do after you been tricked?"

Jonah thought about it. He thought about all de mornings he spent sleepin' under de cypress trees while otha people worked. He thought about how easy it had been to believe Mr. Clarence, because Mr. Clarence told him exactly what he wanted to hear — dat he could do less and still get more.

"I'm gonna work," Jonah said, standin' up. "I'm gonna help replant dis field. And I'm gonna make sure nobody else falls for de same trick."

And dat's exactly what he did.

De community worked together to replant — everyone pitched in, everyone pulled de same rope. De harvest dat year was smaller dan it could have been, but it was honest, and it was shared, and it tasted sweet.

As for Mr. Clarence? Well, he lived in de field for many years after dat, eatin' what he could, hidin' when anyone came near, always one step ahead. He never did get caught, and he never did change. But he also never did have an easy meal again, because Jonah and de others kept de field watchful, and de field was always hungry.

Some say dat if you walk near de old Thompson rice field at dusk, you can still hear a small, crackly voice callin' out from somewhere in de mud: "Hey, long legs! You got a minute? I got a proposition for you…"

And if you're wise, you just keep walkin'.

## Story Notes

**Meaning:** This tale warns against being tricked by those who offer easy solutions to problems we'd rather avoid. Jonah's laziness made him vulnerable to Mr. Clarence's manipulation — the crab offered him a way to appear helpful while doing the minimum work, but in reality, Jonah ended up destroyin' what he was supposed to protect. The story teaches dat shortcuts and avoidance of responsibility often lead to greater harm dan doing the work in de first place.

**Origin:** Dis story originates from de rice-growing communities of de South Carolina lowcountry, particularly around Beaufort and Hilton Head. Rice cultivation was de backbone of de Gullah Geechee economy for centuries, and de relationship between farmers and de natural world — birds, crabs, alligators, tides — was constant and vital. Stories like dis were told to remind young people dat de land demands respect and labor, and dat not every voice you hear is worth listenin' to.

**Cultural Significance:** In Gullah Geechee tradition, animals often serve as tricksters — clever creatures who represent de complexities of human nature. Mr. Clarence is a classic trickster figure, similar to Br'er Rabbit or Anansi in otha African diasporic traditions. De fiddler crab, wid its exaggerated claw, is a real and common sight in coastal marshlands, and its behavior — territorial, opportunistic, always watchful — mirrors de kind of cunning dis story critiques. De tale also reflects de communal values of Gullah Geechee society, where shared labor and shared reward were essential for survival in de challenging lowcountry environment.
