The Words That Survival Taught Us: Preserving Gullah Language

Why every Gullah word we speak is an act of resistance — and how you can help keep them alive

FREE POST  ·  August 31, 2026 ·  387 words
I grew up hearing my grandmother speak a language that most people thought was just "broken English."

She'd say "buh nuh" when she disagreed with something. "Yallow" when someone looked sick. "Winta" when she talked about the cold season. And when she wanted to emphasize something important, she'd say "chi chi" — which meant it was serious.

For years, I thought these were just family expressions. Then I learned they were words with origins in West African languages — Mende, Twi, Yoruba, Wolof — words that had crossed an ocean and survived centuries of attempts to erase them.

The Gullah language is one of the most remarkable linguistic survival stories in American history. While other African American dialects have largely merged with mainstream English, Gullah has retained distinctive vocabulary, grammar, and pronunciation patterns that trace directly back to the languages of the people who were enslaved here.

Here's what I've learned about preserving a language:

**It starts with speaking it.** My grandmother didn't write down her words. She spoke them. To her children. To her grandchildren. To anyone who would listen. Every conversation was an act of preservation.

**It needs documentation.** When elders pass, they take entire worlds of knowledge with them. That's why recording stories, transcribing speeches, and writing things down matters so much.

**It requires context.** A word like "red clay" isn't just soil — it's identity. It's the land that held our ancestors. It's what we come from. When we explain the context, the word becomes meaningful.

**It thrives in community.** Language dies in isolation. It survives when people use it together — at church, at family gatherings, at community events.

The Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor stretches from North Carolina to Florida. Along that corridor, thousands of speakers still use Gullah daily. But the number is shrinking. Every elder who passes takes irreplaceable knowledge with them.

Here's what you can do:

- Record your elders speaking Gullah
- Learn a few words and use them
- Share Gullah language resources
- Support Gullah language preservation organizations
- Read books by Gullah Geechee authors

Every word spoken is a word preserved. Every story told is a story saved.

What Gullah words did your grandmother teach you? Share them in the comments — let's build a living dictionary together.

— Darryl
