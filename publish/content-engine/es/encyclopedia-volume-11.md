# **St. Helena Island: Cultural Heart of the Sea Islands**  

## **Introduction**  

St. Helena Island, a lush expanse of marshland, live oaks, and tidal creeks in the South Carolina Lowcountry, is the cultural and spiritual epicenter of the Gullah Geechee people. For over 300 years, this Sea Island has been home to one of the most intact African-derived cultures in the United States, shaped by the resilience of enslaved Africans and their descendants. Isolated by geography yet connected by tradition, St. Helena became a sanctuary where Gullah Geechee language, spirituality, agriculture, and kinship systems flourished despite the brutalities of slavery and the upheavals of Reconstruction.  

The island’s history is inseparable from the Port Royal Experiment, a Civil War-era initiative that transformed the region into a testing ground for Black freedom and self-governance. At the heart of this transformation was the Penn School, founded in 1862, which became a beacon of education and empowerment. Beyond the school, St. Helena’s landscape tells stories of resistance—from the secret praise houses where enslaved people worshipped, to the landownership that defied Jim Crow dispossession. Today, the island remains a living archive of Gullah Geechee identity, safeguarded by elders, educators, and institutions like the Penn Center.  

This chapter examines St. Helena’s role as the cultural anchor of the Sea Islands, exploring its historical milestones, enduring traditions, and contemporary significance within the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor.  

---

## **1. The Geography of Memory: St. Helena’s Landscape and Legacy**  

St. Helena’s physical terrain—fertile soil, brackish rivers, and barrier islands—shaped its cultural and economic life. Enslaved Africans, brought to work the indigo and cotton plantations of the 18th century, adapted West African rice-growing techniques to the island’s tidal plains, creating the foundation for the region’s agricultural wealth. Place names like **Frogmore** and **Land’s End** still echo the Gullah language, while ancient burial grounds, marked by seashells and broken pottery, honor ancestors through African-derived rituals.  

After the Civil War, St. Helena became a rare haven for Black landownership. The 1865 **Special Field Orders No. 15** temporarily granted freedpeople "40 acres and a mule," and many Gullah Geechee families pooled resources to buy parcels through **the Freedmen’s Bureau**. By 1900, over 60% of the island was Black-owned, a stark contrast to the sharecropping systems dominating the South. This autonomy allowed traditions like **task-based labor** (a West African work model) and **communal fishing** to endure well into the 20th century.  

---

## **2. The Penn School: Education as Liberation**  

As detailed in earlier volumes, the Penn School (1862–1948) was a revolutionary force in Gullah Geechee education. However, its impact was deeply intertwined with St. Helena’s social fabric. Under the leadership of **Laura Towne** and **Ellen Murray**, the school’s curriculum blended academic rigor with cultural preservation:  

- **Language**: Students learned English but were not punished for speaking Gullah, a policy rare among Freedmen’s schools.  
- **Music and Storytelling**: Spirituals like "Kumbaya" (derived from the Gullah phrase "Come By Here") were taught alongside European hymns.  
- **Land Advocacy**: Teachers helped families navigate **heirs’ property** laws to prevent land loss—a legacy continued today by the **Penn Center’s Land Ownership Project**.  

Oral histories from alumni, like **Marquetta Goodwine (Queen Quet)**, emphasize how the school instilled pride in Gullah identity despite outside pressures to assimilate.  

---

## **3. Spirituality and the Invisible Institution: Praise Houses and Folkways**  

St. Helena’s spiritual life reflects a fusion of Christianity and African retentions. Enslaved people worshipped in **"praise houses"**—small, secluded structures where they practiced **ring shouts**, a devotional dance of West African origin. After emancipation, these traditions thrived in churches like **Brick Baptist** (1855) and **Beaufort’s Tabernacle Baptist**, where Gullah Geechee ministers incorporated call-and-response preaching and **"seekin’" rituals** (spiritual quests for divine calling).  

Rootwork and herbalism, known as **"gullah doctoring,"** also persist. Elders like **Maum Chrish’** (a famed midwife) passed down knowledge of local remedies—using **life-everlasting** for colds or **bull nettle** for arthritis—blending Bantu and Cherokee healing practices.  

---

## **4. The Civil Rights Movement: A Safe Harbor for Change**  

In the 1960s, St. Helena’s isolation made it a strategic sanctuary for civil rights organizers. The **Penn Center** hosted **Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.** and the **Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC)**, who planned protests and trained activists in nonviolent resistance. **Septima Clark**, a St. Helena native, leveraged the island’s networks to launch **Citizenship Schools**, teaching literacy to Black voters to circumvent Jim Crow literacy tests.  

Local residents risked retaliation to support the movement. **Robert Smalls**, a Civil War hero and Reconstruction congressman, had earlier exemplified this courage; his descendants continued advocating for voting rights well into the 20th century.  

---

## **5. Cultural Preservation in the 21st Century: Challenges and Triumphs**  

Modern St. Helena balances tradition and change. Threats like **tourism-driven development** and **climate change** (eroding ancestral lands) clash with efforts to preserve heritage. Key initiatives include:  

- **The Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor**: Established in 2006, it protects historic sites and supports artisan economies.  
- **The St. Helena Island Cultural Protection Overlay**: A zoning law preventing resorts and gated communities, passed after protests against a proposed golf course on historic **Hepburn Cemetery**.  
- **Digital Archives**: Projects like **"Lowcountry Africana"** document family histories and endangered crafts like sweetgrass basket-weaving.  

Elders remain vocal. In 2022, **Rev. Dr. Jonny McCollough** told the *Island Packet*, "If we lose this land, we lose our roots. Gullah ain’t a performance—it’s a way of life."  

---

## **Conclusion**  

St. Helena Island’s story is one of unbroken resilience. From the first Africans who carved rice fields from the marshes, to the children of the Penn School, to the activists who gathered in its shade, the island has nurtured a distinct cultural sovereignty. Its landscapes, language, and lifeways testify to the ingenuity of the Gullah Geechee people—a living rebuttal to narratives of erasure. As development pressures mount, St. Helena’s fate will hinge on whether America values preservation over profit. For now, it stands as the soul of the Sea Islands, whispering its history in the wind through the oaks.
