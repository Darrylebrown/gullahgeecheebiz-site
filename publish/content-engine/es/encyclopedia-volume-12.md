# **Gullah Geechee Contributions to American Culture**  

## **Introduction**  

The Gullah Geechee people, descendants of enslaved Africans who worked the rice, indigo, and cotton plantations along the coastal regions of South Carolina, Georgia, Florida, and North Carolina, have preserved a distinct cultural heritage that has profoundly influenced American society. Their language, cuisine, music, crafts, and oral traditions retain strong African retentions due to their relative isolation on the Sea Islands. Despite systemic oppression and displacement, the Gullah Geechee have contributed significantly to the cultural fabric of the United States, particularly in the realms of music, foodways, agriculture, linguistic studies, and civil rights activism. This chapter explores these contributions, highlighting key moments, figures, and traditions that illustrate the resilience and creativity of the Gullah Geechee people.  

## **1. The Gullah Geechee Language: A Living Link to Africa**  

The Gullah language, sometimes referred to as Geechee, is an English-based creole language with heavy West and Central African linguistic influences, particularly from languages such as Mende, Wolof, Mandinka, and Kikongo. Scholars such as Lorenzo Dow Turner (1895-1972) pioneered the study of Gullah, documenting its African linguistic roots in his seminal work *Africanisms in the Gullah Dialect* (1949). Turner’s research proved that Gullah was not "broken English" but a sophisticated creole with distinct grammar, syntax, and vocabulary.  

Gullah’s influence extends beyond linguistics; Gullah storytelling traditions, such as the Br'er Rabbit tales (later appropriated in Joel Chandler Harris’s *Uncle Remus* stories), originated in African oral traditions. Contemporary Gullah speakers, like Queen Quet (Marquetta L. Goodwine), advocate for language preservation, ensuring that future generations retain this vital cultural inheritance.  

## **2. Culinary Legacy: The Gullah Geechee Influence on Southern Cuisine**  

Gullah Geechee cuisine is foundational to what is now considered "Southern food." Dishes such as okra soup, hoppin' John (a rice and pea dish), shrimp and grits, and gumbo trace their origins to West African cooking techniques and ingredients. Enslaved Africans introduced rice cultivation—critical to South Carolina’s economy—along with knowledge of tidal irrigation, an innovation that revolutionized Southern agriculture.  

Chefs and culinary historians like Vertamae Smart-Grosvenor (1937-2016) emphasized Gullah foodways in her book *Vibration Cooking: or, The Travel Notes of a Geechee Girl* (1970), which challenged Eurocentric culinary narratives. Today, chefs like Benjamin “BJ” Dennis and Charlotte Jenkins promote Gullah Geechee cuisine in fine dining and food education, ensuring its recognition as a cornerstone of American food culture.  

## **3. Musical Traditions: From Ring Shouts to Hip-Hop**  

Gullah Geechee musical traditions have deeply shaped American music. The ring shout—a religious dance accompanied by call-and-response singing—is one of the earliest African American musical forms and a precursor to gospel, blues, and jazz. The call-and-response pattern, polyrhythms, and improvisational styles in Gullah music influenced artists across genres.  

The spiritual "Kumbaya," meaning "Come By Here" in Gullah, became an international folk anthem. Lowcountry artists like Bessie Jones (1902-1984) of the Georgia Sea Island Singers preserved these traditions, while contemporary musicians like Darius Rucker (Hootie & the Blowfish) reflect Gullah heritage in their work. Even hip-hop, with its storytelling and rhythmic complexity, bears traces of Gullah oral traditions.  

## **4. Craftsmanship and Sweetgrass Basketry**  

Gullah Geechee artisanship, particularly sweetgrass basket weaving, is a celebrated African survival. Originating in Sierra Leone, these coiled baskets were originally used for rice winnowing. Today, they are sold as high-end art, with weavers like Mary Jackson (1945- ) elevating the craft to national recognition (she received a MacArthur "Genius" Fellowship in 2008).  

The craft is also economically vital; vendors in Charleston’s City Market and Mount Pleasant sustain family traditions passed down for generations. Efforts by the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor (established 2006) help preserve these artisanal practices amidst urbanization and cultural erasure.  

## **5. Civil Rights and Cultural Activism**  

Gullah Geechee leaders have long fought for civil rights and cultural preservation. Robert Smalls (1839-1915), born enslaved in Beaufort, South Carolina, commandeered a Confederate ship to escape slavery, later becoming a U.S. Congressman. Septima Poinsette Clark (1898-1987), known as the "Mother of the Civil Rights Movement," developed literacy programs that empowered Black voters.  

Modern activists like Emory Campbell (1941-2021) of Penn Center and Queen Quet advocate for Gullah Geechee land rights against gentrification. Their work ensures that development does not erase their communities, as seen in struggles like the fight against Hilton Head’s displacement of Gullah families in the 20th century.  

## **Conclusion**  

The Gullah Geechee people have made indelible contributions to American culture, from language and music to food and civil rights. Their cultural retention is not merely historical but a living tradition that continues to evolve. As scholars and community leaders work against assimilation pressures, the Gullah Geechee legacy stands as a testament to resilience, creativity, and the enduring influence of African heritage in America.
