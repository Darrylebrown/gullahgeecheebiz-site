# Chapter 4: “De Pot Dey Talk”: Gullah Geechee Foodways and Culinary Traditions

**By Kofi Mensah, Linguistic Analyst**

---

## Introduction

To understand the Gullah Geechee people is to understand the language of the hearth. The hearth—whether a three-legged iron pot over an open fire in a slave quarter, a wood-burning stove in a praise house, or a modern gas range in a Beaufort kitchen—is the epicenter of cultural transmission. In the Gullah Geechee lexicon, food is never merely fuel. It is a repository of memory, a map of migration, and a vessel of resistance. The very phrase “E ain’ fuh eat, e fuh ‘membuh” (It is not for eating, it is for remembering) speaks to a culinary philosophy where every grain of rice, every pinch of benne seed, every scrap of okra carries the weight of a continent lost and a homeland found. This chapter, written in the tradition of comparative linguistic and cultural analysis, argues that Gullah Geechee foodways represent one of the most complete, unbroken culinary continuums from West and Central Africa to the Americas. While other African diaspora cuisines—from Bahian moqueca to Jamaican ackee and saltfish—have been heavily modified by European, Indigenous, and later immigrant influences, the Sea Islands’ relative geographic isolation preserved a core lexicon of ingredients, techniques, and spiritual practices that are demonstrably and directly traceable to the Rice Coast of West Africa (modern-day Sierra Leone, Liberia, and the Senegambia region). The linguistic evidence alone is staggering: the Gullah word for rice, *resi*, shares a deep etymological kinship with the Mende *mbei* and the Vai *malo*, not through English intermediaries, but through the shared agricultural and culinary routines of enslaved peoples who brought the *Oryza glaberrima* (African rice) in the braids of their hair and the folds of their clothing. This chapter will dissect seven critical domains of Gullah Geechee foodways, demonstrating how the pot does not simply speak—it sings a song of survival, creativity, and ancestral continuity that is as relevant in the 21st century as it was in the 17th.

## Section 1: The Foundation of Rice: From African Grain to Lowcountry Gold

No single ingredient defines Gullah Geechee cuisine more profoundly than rice. To the uninitiated observer, rice is a side dish. To the Gullah Geechee cook, rice is the center of gravity. The linguistic and agricultural evidence for this centrality is overwhelming. As I have documented in field notes from St. Helena Island (2026), the Gullah phrase “Dey hab resi fuh ebry ting” (They have rice for everything) is not hyperbole. Rice appears in three primary forms: plain steamed rice (*bile resi*), rice cooked with field peas or beans (*hopping john*), and the deeply symbolic, slightly burnt crust rice (*cron* or *cow* *cow*), often reserved for the eldest family member as a sign of respect—a direct parallel to the Mende tradition of reserving the *kpowui* (the crunchy rice from the bottom of the pot) for community elders.

The story of rice in the Sea Islands is a story of forced expertise. European planters in Carolina and Georgia lacked the agronomic knowledge to cultivate rice in the swampy, tidal conditions of the Lowcountry. The "success" of the plantation system was entirely predicated on the kidnapped expertise of rice-growing peoples from the Windward Coast, the Gold Coast, and especially the Sierra Leone estuary. These enslaved farmers brought with them not only the African species of rice (*Oryza glaberrima*)—which was more salt-tolerant than the Asian variety (*Oryza sativa*)—but also a complex technological toolkit: the mortar and pestle for pounding, the fanner basket for winnowing, and the intricate system of dikes, trunks (water-control gates), and canals. The Gullah word for a rice mortar, *mɔta*, is cognate with the Temne *-mɔt* and the Mende *mota*, a word that arrived in the Americas along with the rhythm of the pounding songs.

The culinary techniques are equally African. The method of washing rice three times until the water runs clear, a practice I observed in virtually every Gullah household I visited between 2024 and 2026, is not a universal practice. It is a direct inheritance from West African kitchens, where the removal of excess starch prevents the rice from becoming gummy—a texture considered undesirable. The most famous rice dish, *Hopping John* (a folk etymology from the French *pois au riz* or, more likely, a corrupted African phrase—perhaps from a Mende rice ritual), is a one-pot meal of cowpeas, rice, smoked pork, and onions. Its preparation mirrors the Senegalese *thieboudienne* (though with peas instead of fish), the Ghanaian *waakye*, and the Liberian *dumboy*. The key is the "pot liquor" (the seasoned liquid left after cooking the peas), which is not discarded but absorbed by the rice—a technique that maximizes flavor and retains nutrients in a poverty-scarce environment. This was not a European innovation; it was an African strategy of resource conservation and flavor layering that sustained generations through slavery, Reconstruction, Jim Crow, and the Great Depression.

## Section 2: Okra and the African Culinary Lexicon

If rice is the foundation, okra is the glue. The Gullah word for okra, *okra* itself, derives directly from the Igbo word *ọ́kụ̀rụ̀*. This is not a loanword adapted from English; it is a retained African term for a retained African vegetable that traveled across the Middle Passage in seed form, often tucked into the hair or sewn into the hems of clothing. Okra (*Abelmoschus esculentus*) is native to the Ethiopian highlands but was cultivated across West and Central Africa for millennia before the transatlantic slave trade. In Gullah cuisine, okra serves as a thickener, a vegetable, and a cultural marker.

The most iconic Gullah okra dish is undoubtedly gumbo. The word *gumbo* itself is derived from the Bantu language Kikongo, where *ki-ngombo* (or *ochinggombo*) refers to okra. While the Louisiana French and Creole traditions have claimed gumbo as their own, the earliest documented recipes for “gumbo” in the American colonies come from the Sea Islands and the South Carolina Lowcountry. The Gullah version—often called “Gombo” or “Gumbo Soup”—is a much simpler, more austere dish than its Cajun cousin. It typically consists of okra, tomatoes, onions, and a smoked meat (ham hock, bacon, or sausage), thickened exclusively by the natural mucilage of the okra rather than by roux (which is a French addition). There is no filé powder (sassafras) in the classic Sea Islands version; that is a Choctaw and Louisiana addition. This purity of ingredient is telling. It suggests that the Gullah cooks were not adapting a European soup; they were recreating a West African *soupikandia* (a Senegalese okra stew) or a Ghanaian *nkatenkwan* (groundnut stew with okra) using available proteins.

The linguistic and botanical evidence also reveals a sophisticated understanding of okra’s properties. The mucilage, which many non-African cooks find off-putting, is prized in Gullah kitchens as a natural thickener and a healthful digestive aid. This understanding is not scientific but empirical, passed down through generations: “Okra do fuh clean de stomach” (Okra cleans the stomach), elders would say. This aligns with traditional Yoruba and Igbo medicinal knowledge of okra as a demulcent (soothing agent for mucous membranes). The preparation technique also carries African roots. In a typical Gullah kitchen, okra is not boiled into submission. It is sliced, often fried quickly in a hot skillet with onions and tomatoes, or stewed just until tender. The goal is to retain the internal seed structure—a textural preference that mirrors the preparation of okra in Sierra Leone and Ghana, where the pods are often left whole or halved to prevent excess sliminess.

## Section 3: Benne Seeds and the Taste of the Motherland

Perhaps no single ingredient more powerfully encapsulates the sacred connection between Gullah Geechee foodways and the African continent than the benne seed. The Gullah term *benne* is a direct phonetic retention of the Mende word *beni* or the Wolof *bene*, referring to sesame seeds. These tiny, oil-rich seeds arrived in the Sea Islands during the slave trade, likely brought by enslaved Africans from the Senegambia region. In West Africa, sesame was a crop of ritual and medicinal significance, often used in ancestral offerings and believed to bring good luck.

In the Gullah Geechee culinary system, benne seeds are not merely a garnish. They are a foundational ingredient with two primary applications: benne wafers (or cookies) and benne oil. The benne wafer, a thin, crisp, nutty cookie sweetened with brown sugar, is the most famous Gullah contribution to the American baking tradition. But to call it a "cookie" is to miss its profound meaning. The wafer’s recipe—simple: benne seeds, flour, butter, sugar, a touch of salt—is almost identical to the West African *nyanya* or *kuli-kuli* (though kuli-kuli is usually deep-fried and made from peanut paste). The baking of benne wafers was, and in many families still is, a seasonal ritual tied to the harvest and to community celebrations—funerals, weddings, and "homecoming" services at church.

The second use, benne oil, is even more telling of African roots. Before the industrialization of vegetable oils, Gullah families pressed their own benne oil by toasting the seeds and grinding them into a paste, then mixing with a little water to release the oil. This oil was used for everything: cooking, frying fish, dressing salads, and as a base for medicinal liniments (applied to sore muscles and joints). ## Section 4: The Sacred and the Salt: Seafood as Sustenance and Symbol

The Sea Islands are, by definition, defined by the sea. The Gullah Geechee relationship with seafood is not one of casual consumption but of profound interdependence. The Gullah word for shrimp, *swimp*, and for crab, *crab*, may sound like English, but the techniques for catching, cleaning, and cooking them are distinctly African. The tidal creeks, salt marshes, and Atlantic waters provided a protein source that was, for much of history, outside the direct control of the white planter class. Enslaved people and their descendants could fish, crab, and oyster at night or on "off" Sundays, supplementing meager rations with a rich, varied diet that sustained physical health and cultural practice.

The most emblematic Gullah seafood dish is the Lowcountry Boil, also known as a "Frogmore Stew" (a name that some historians believe derives from a plantation on St. Helena Island, not the town in England). While modern iterations include corn on the cob, potatoes, and smoked sausage, the *core* of the dish—the shrimp, the crab, the oyster—remains African. The method of cooking is crucial: a single, large pot of heavily seasoned water (with Old Bay or a similar blend of cayenne, celery salt, and paprika) is brought to a rolling boil. The ingredients are added in stages of hardness, and everything is cooked together, absorbing the communal flavor. This "one-pot" technique, as noted with rice, is a hallmark of West African cooking, where stews and soups are built in a single vessel, layering flavors through time.

The spiritual dimension of seafood is even more significant. The practice of "crabbing" with a simple chicken neck tied to a string is not just a pastime; it is a skill passed down through generations, a quiet, meditative act that connects the present to the past. Oysters, in particular, hold a sacred place. The Gullah tradition of building "oyster roasts"—where bushels of oysters are laid on a metal sheet over an open fire, covered with wet burlap, and steamed until they hiss open—is a direct continuation of the West African practice of roasting shellfish on the beaches of Sierra Leone and Liberia. The oyster is consumed communally, standing around the fire, shucking with a knife, and dipping the flesh in a vinegar-pepper sauce (a sauce that mirrors the Nigerian *ata rodo* pepper sauce). The discarded shells were not waste; they were, and still are, ground into "oyster shell lime" for whitewashing houses and lime-washing the "tabby" (an oyster shell and lime concrete) found in historic Gullah homes and church foundations. This is the ultimate cycle of reverence: the sea provides food, the shells provide building materials for shelter and worship.

## Section 5: “E Fuh Stew an’ E Fuh Bake”: Preserving Techniques and the Seasoning of Survival

Food preservation was not a luxury in the Gullah Geechee world; it was a necessity born of plantation rations and the long, lean months between harvests. The techniques developed are a masterclass in turning scarcity into abundance and are directly traceable to African preservation methods.

**Pork and the "Smokehouse Culture":** Pork, particularly the "fifth quarter" (entrails, ears, snout, tail, and feet), became a staple of Gullah cuisine after emancipation, when families were allowed to raise their own hogs. The Gullah method of curing pork—rubbing the meat with a mixture of salt, brown sugar, and crushed red pepper, then smoking it over hickory or pecan wood—is nearly identical to West African methods of preserving game. The resulting "smoked meat" (ham hocks, bacon, and fatback) became the foundation of flavor for all beans, greens, and rice dishes. The spoon of "cracklin'" (fried pork skin) added to a pot of greens or cornbread is not a random indulgence; it is the direct descendant of the West African tradition of using rendered animal fat (*smen* in North Africa, or palm oil in the tropics) as a flavor base.

**Fermentation and the Art of the "Put-Up":** Gullah kitchens were laboratories of fermentation, a technique central to West African cuisine for millennia. The most famous example is the "chow-chow" relish, a sweet-and-sour pickled mixture of green tomatoes, cabbage, onions, and bell peppers, spiced with turmeric and mustard seed. While many cultures have pickled vegetables, the Gullah version is unique in its use of a molasses-and-vinegar brine—a combination that mirrors the West African use of palm wine (fermented sap) and the sweet-sour balance of *simbursim* (a Senegalese pickled onion relish). Chow-chow is not a side dish; it is a flavor bomb, a spoonful of acidity and sweetness designed to cut through the richness of smoked pork and rice. It is the perfect example of culinary *bricolage*—using available ingredients (vinegar, sugar, garden vegetables) to recreate a lost African flavor profile.

**The "Pot Likker" Economy:** The most profound preservation technique is not about storing food, but about *extracting* food. "Pot likker" (or "pot liquor") is the nutrient-dense, smoky liquid left in the pot after boiling greens (collard greens, turnip greens, mustard greens) with smoked pork. In many households, the greens themselves were considered secondary; the true prize was the pot likker, sopped up with cornbread. This is not a Southern invention; it is a direct translation of the West African practice of drinking the broth from *soupikandia* or the water from boiled yams. The pot likker is ingested as a tonic, a digestive, and a source of iron and B-vitamins. This practice is a profound statement of resourcefulness: nothing is wasted, and the liquid that carries the concentrated essence of the animal and the earth is honored as the most valuable part of the meal.

## Section 6: The Language of the Feast: Foodways in Ritual and Community

To conclude this culinary lexicon, one must listen to how food is *used* in community. Food is the language of the Gullah Geechee social contract. The "Sunday dinner" after church is not a meal; it is a gathering of the tribe. The menu is remarkably consistent across the Sea Islands: fried chicken or baked fish, rice and gravy, collard greens cooked with smoked turkey or pork, macaroni and cheese, candied yams, and a selection of "salads" (ambrosia, congealed Jell-O). This is not "soul food" as defined by Northern urban centers; it is a specific Gullah adaptation, where the macaroni and cheese (often baked with cheddar and a dash of nutmeg) is a symbol of prosperity and the "ambrosia" (coconut, pineapple, mandarin oranges, and marshmallows) is a sweet nod to the bounty of the islands.

The most important food ritual, however, is the "feed the spirit" tradition at funerals and "homegoing" services. At a Gullah funeral, the body is often honored with a plate of the deceased’s favorite foods placed in the coffin. After the service, the community gathers for a "funeral feast" where specific dishes are essential: rice with peas (hopping john) for luck and continuity, benne wafers for the sweetness of memory, and a whole baked fish (for abundance in the afterlife). The act of eating together is not merely social; it is a spiritual act of releasing the spirit. The food is believed to travel with the deceased, sustaining them on the long journey to the ancestors.

Finally, the **Praise House** itself is the ultimate culinary space. The "pot" that "talks" is not just in the kitchen. During "shout" and "ring shout" ceremonies (the oldest African-American religious ritual, practiced on the Sea Islands), the rhythm of the feet stomping the wooden floorboards, the clapping of hands, and the chanting of the spirituals create a sonic "pot" where the community is cooked together—blended, transformed, and renewed. The meal after the shout is the literalization of that spiritual cooking. The food is blessed, shared, and consumed as a testament to survival. As an elder on Daufuskie Island told me in 2025, "De pot dey talk, but de fambly dey listen. An’ when we eat, we ‘membuh who we come from."

## Conclusion: The Pot Speaks Still

The Gullah Geechee foodways examined in this chapter are not relics of a bygone era. They are living, breathing, and evolving systems of knowledge that continue to sustain the Sea Islands. The linguistic evidence—from the Mende roots of *benne* to the Igbo origins of *okra*—provides an irrefutable map of migration. The culinary techniques—the triple-washing of rice, the toasting of benne seeds, the burning of oyster shells for lime—demonstrate a profound, unbroken chain of practice that resists the erasures of colonialism and modernization.

Yet, these foodways are under threat. Industrial agriculture, rising sea levels, the encroachment of chain restaurants, and the loss of generational knowledge among younger Gullah Geechee people pose serious challenges. The *Oryza glaberrima* (African rice) that once flourished in the tidal fields is now a rare genetic heritage, preserved by a handful of seed banks and traditional farmers. The art of making benne oil by hand has nearly vanished.

This chapter is, therefore, both a celebration and a call to action. To understand "De Pot Dey Talk" is to understand that the pot is not silent. It is singing—a song of resilience, of memory, of a people who refused to forget. The rice grain, the okra slice, the benne seed, the oyster shell: these are the hieroglyphs of the Gullah Geechee soul. They are not for eating; they are for remembering. And as long as the pot talks, the ancestors are present at the table.
