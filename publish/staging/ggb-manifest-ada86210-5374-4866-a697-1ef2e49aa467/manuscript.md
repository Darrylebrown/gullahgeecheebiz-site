# Environmental Justice in Gullah Geechee Communities  
*Volume 47 of the Gullah Geechee Encyclopedia Series*  

---

## Introduction  

The Gullah Geechee people, descendants of enslaved Africans brought to the coastal regions of the Southeastern United States, have cultivated a unique cultural and linguistic heritage deeply intertwined with the land and waterways of their ancestral homes. Spanning from coastal North Carolina to northern Florida, the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor encompasses a landscape that has sustained generations of Gullah Geechee families. However, this relationship with the environment has been severely strained by systemic inequities, environmental degradation, and development pressures that threaten both their cultural preservation and environmental sovereignty.  

Environmental justice, the principle that all communities deserve equitable access to a healthy environment and protection from environmental harms, is a critical lens through which to examine these issues. For Gullah Geechee communities, environmental justice is not only about addressing pollution or land loss but also about preserving cultural identity, affirming land rights, and ensuring intergenerational resilience. This chapter explores the historical and contemporary challenges faced by Gullah Geechee communities in achieving environmental justice, highlighting their resistance, resilience, and advocacy efforts.  

---

## Historical Context: Land, Dispossession, and Resource Exploitation  

### The Legacy of Enslavement and Post-Emancipation Land Ownership  

The Gullah Geechee people’s ancestral connection to the coastal Southeast began during the transatlantic slave trade, when enslaved Africans were forcibly brought to work on rice, indigo, and cotton plantations. Their agricultural expertise, honed in West Africa, made them invaluable to these industries. Following emancipation, many Gullah Geechee families acquired land through Reconstruction-era land grants or purchase, establishing small, self-sustaining communities.  

However, this land ownership was precarious. Systemic racism, discriminatory policies, and economic exploitation often forced Gullah Geechee families to sell their land. The U.S. government’s seizure of land for military bases during World War II, such as the establishment of Marine Corps Recruit Depot Parris Island in South Carolina, displaced entire communities. These historical injustices laid the groundwork for contemporary environmental challenges.  

### Industrial Exploitation and Pollution  

In the 20th century, industrial development further encroached on Gullah Geechee lands. Factories, chemical plants, and tourism projects polluted waterways and degraded ecosystems essential to traditional livelihoods like fishing and farming. For example, the Savannah River Site, a nuclear production facility near Beaufort County, South Carolina, has been linked to pollution that affects nearby Gullah Geechee communities.  

---

## Land Loss and Climate Change  

### Erosion and Sea Level Rise  

The Gullah Geechee Corridor is particularly vulnerable to climate change, with sea level rise and coastal erosion threatening ancestral lands. In places like Hog Hammock on Sapelo Island, Georgia, and James Island, South Carolina, rising waters and stronger storms have accelerated land loss, forcing families to relocate.  

### Development Pressures  

Rapid coastal development exacerbates these challenges. Luxury resorts, golf courses, and gated communities often displace Gullah Geechee families, fragmenting communities and eroding cultural landscapes. The loss of land undermines traditional practices, such as farming, fishing, and communal gatherings, that depend on access to shared spaces.  

---

## Cultural Preservation and Environmental Sovereignty  

### Traditional Ecological Knowledge  

Gullah Geechee communities possess rich traditional ecological knowledge (TEK) that has sustained their environments for centuries. Practices like tidal rice cultivation, sustainable fishing, and the use of medicinal plants reflect a deep understanding of local ecosystems. However, TEK is at risk as environmental changes and land loss disrupt these practices.  

### Language and Storytelling  

The Gullah Geechee language, a creole influenced by West African languages and English, is a vital part of their cultural heritage. Stories, songs, and oral histories often encode environmental wisdom, such as warnings about storms or guidance for planting crops. Preserving this language is intertwined with preserving environmental knowledge.  

---

## Grassroots Advocacy and Legal Battles  

### Community-Led Resistance  

Gullah Geechee communities have a long history of grassroots advocacy. Organizations like the Gullah Geechee Sea Island Coalition, founded by Marquetta Goodwine (Queen Quet), and the Lowcountry Gullah Foundation work to protect land rights, promote cultural preservation, and advocate for environmental justice.  

### Legal Challenges  

Legal battles have been central to these efforts. The 2005 Supreme Court case *Kelo v. City of New London* highlighted the vulnerability of Gullah Geechee landowners to eminent domain abuse. More recently, lawsuits have been filed to challenge pollution and development projects that threaten Gullah Geechee communities.  

---

## Intersectional Approaches to Environmental Justice  

### Health Disparities  

Environmental injustices often manifest as health disparities. Polluted air and water contribute to higher rates of asthma, cancer, and other illnesses in Gullah Geechee communities. Addressing these disparities requires holistic, intersectional approaches that prioritize both environmental and public health.  

### Education and Empowerment  

Efforts to empower Gullah Geechee youth are crucial for sustaining environmental justice. Programs that teach TEK, environmental science, and advocacy skills help ensure that future generations can continue advocating for their communities.  

---

## Conclusion  

Environmental justice in Gullah Geechee communities is a multifaceted issue that intersects with cultural preservation, land rights, and climate resilience. Historical injustices, systemic inequities, and contemporary challenges have disproportionately burdened these communities, threatening their way of life. However, through grassroots advocacy, legal battles, and the preservation of traditional knowledge, Gullah Geechee communities continue to resist environmental degradation and fight for their rights.  

As the world grapples with climate change and environmental inequities, the struggles and triumphs of the Gullah Geechee people offer valuable lessons in resilience, sustainability, and the importance of centering marginalized voices in environmental justice movements.
