# **Land Loss and Development Pressure in the Sea Islands**  

## **Introduction**  

The Sea Islands—a chain of barrier islands and lowcountry regions stretching from North Carolina to Florida—have long been the cultural heartland of the Gullah Geechee people, descendants of enslaved Africans who developed a distinct Creole language, culture, and agricultural lifeway. Since Emancipation, Gullah Geechee communities have maintained strong ties to the land through traditions of communal ownership, subsistence farming, and fishing. However, since the mid-20th century, these communities have faced escalating threats from land dispossession, discriminatory policies, and rapid coastal development. Today, unchecked tourism, climate change, and luxury real estate speculation intensify pressures on ancestral lands, jeopardizing cultural heritage and community survival. This chapter examines the historical roots of land loss, systemic forces driving displacement, and ongoing resistance efforts led by Gullah Geechee advocates.  

## **1. The Foundation of Gullah Geechee Land Ownership**  

After the Civil War, the federal government’s “40 Acres and a Mule” promise briefly materialized in the Sea Islands through General William T. Sherman’s Special Field Orders No. 15 (1865), which redistributed confiscated Confederate land to freedmen. On islands like St. Helena, Edisto, and Hilton Head, formerly enslaved people acquired land through direct purchase or informal communal arrangements. Heirs’ property—a system where land passes informally among descendants without clear legal title—became a cornerstone of Gullah Geechee land retention, ensuring intergenerational continuity. However, this lack of formal documentation would later render families vulnerable to exploitation.  

Oral histories from the Penn Center on St. Helena Island attest to early self-sufficiency: “We raised our own food—corn, sweet potatoes, collards—and fished the rivers. Nobody could move us, because we owned the land” (Campbell, 2008).  

## **2. 20th-Century Dispossession: Taxes, Developers, and Legal Loopholes**  

Between the 1920s and 1960s, systemic mechanisms eroded Black landownership. Southern states imposed discriminatory tax assessments, forcing families to sell when they couldn’t pay inflated bills. White developers and speculators exploited heirs’ property laws by pressuring one descendant to sell, triggering legal partitions that dispossessed entire families. On Daufuskie Island (SC), Black landowners lost thousands of acres to predatory buyers offering quick cash.  

The 1956 Highway Act disproportionately targeted Black communities, using eminent domain to seize prime coastal land for bridges and highways, facilitating tourist access (Colleton, 2010). Meanwhile, segregation-era banks systematically denied loans to Black farmers, crippling economic resilience.  

## **3. Boom and Bust: The Resort Takeover of the Sea Islands**  

By the 1970s, developers transformed the Sea Islands into luxury destinations. Hilton Head Island, once home to thriving Gullah settlements like Mitchelville, saw golf courses and gated communities replace farmland. Prominent Gullah landowners like Santee Lourie Gould fought unsuccessfully to block rezoning (Jones-Jackson, 1987). Similar trends occurred on St. Simons (GA) and Kiawah (SC), where beachfront “plantations” marketing antebellum nostalgia erased Black presence.  

Gullah families who resisted buyouts faced harassment. On Sapelo Island (GA), descendants of Hog Hammock recount surveillance and pressure to sell: “They didn’t want us here. They called our land ‘undeveloped’—but we were using it just fine” (Bailey, 2012).  

## **4. Climate Change and the Double Threat of Erosion and Displacement**  

Rising sea levels and hurricanes now compound dispossession. On Johns Island (SC), saltwater intrusion ruins farmland, while insurers redline Black homeowners, devaluing property before corporate buyouts. After Hurricane Matthew (2016), developers seized flooded lots under the guise of “blight removal” (Smithsonian Folklife, 2020).  

Meanwhile, disaster aid favors wealthy landowners. A Gullah activist in Beaufort noted, “We rebuild by hand, while new condos get state funding” (Coastal Heritage Magazine, 2019).  

## **5. Resistance and Legal Battles: The Fight to Stay**  

Grassroots coalitions like the Gullah/Geechee Land & Culture Preservation Task Force and the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor advocate for policy reforms. Key victories include:  
- **Heirs’ Property Reform:** South Carolina’s 2006 law simplified title resolution.  
- **Zoning Challenges:** On St. Helena, community organizing blocked a mega-resort in 2021.  
- **Land Trusts:** The Gullah Geechee Community Development Corporation acquires and protects ancestral land.  

Elder Esau Jenkins of Johns Island famously declared, “We must teach the young to hold on” (PBS, 2017). Today, youth-led oral history projects document family land narratives as legal evidence.  

## **Conclusion**  

The Sea Islands’ fate hinges on whether Gullah Geechee stewardship is prioritized over extractive development. While legislative tools and activism slow displacement, structural inequities persist. Centering Gullah Geechee sovereignty—through land trusts, historic designations, and reparative policies—offers a path toward justice. As scholar Vertamae Grosvenor asserts, “This land is our archive” (Gullah Roots, 2015). Its survival is non-negotiable.
