# **The Port Royal Experiment and Land Ownership**  
*From the Gullah Geechee Encyclopedia, Volume 17*  

## **Introduction**  
The Port Royal Experiment (1862–1865) was one of the most significant early efforts in Reconstruction-era policy, testing the feasibility of Black landownership, education, and self-governance in the South. Following the Union’s capture of the Sea Islands of South Carolina—home to a large population of Gullah Geechee people—formerly enslaved Africans seized the opportunity to claim autonomy over the lands they had worked for generations. This chapter examines the experiment’s impact on land ownership, tracing how Gullah Geechee communities resisted displacement, negotiated labor contracts, and fought for legal title to their ancestral lands. Through firsthand accounts, government records, and oral histories, we explore the successes, failures, and enduring legacy of this pivotal moment in Gullah Geechee history.  

## **1. The Union Occupation and the Dawn of Freedom**  
In November 1861, Union forces captured Port Royal Sound and the surrounding Sea Islands, prompting white plantation owners to flee while leaving behind thousands of enslaved Africans. These newly freed people, primarily Gullah Geechee speakers, remained on the land, refusing to abandon their homes despite the chaos of war. Recognizing the strategic and humanitarian opportunity, Northern abolitionists, missionaries, and government officials launched the Port Royal Experiment—a social and economic initiative aimed at transitioning the region from slavery to free labor and Black landownership.  

Key figures included:  
- **Edward L. Pierce** – A lawyer and abolitionist who organized early relief efforts.  
- **Laura Towne & Charlotte Forten** – Northern educators who founded the Penn School on St. Helena Island, a cornerstone of Gullah Geechee education.  
- **General Rufus Saxton** – The military governor who championed Black land redistribution.  

Freedpeople quickly asserted their independence, refusing to work under exploitative conditions and demanding fair wages. Their resistance laid the groundwork for later land claims.  

## **2. Sherman’s Special Field Order No. 15 and the Promise of "40 Acres and a Mule"**  
On January 16, 1865, General William T. Sherman issued **Special Field Order No. 15**, designating 400,000 acres of confiscated coastal land from Charleston to northern Florida for redistribution to freed Black families. Each family was to receive "not more than forty acres" of tillable land, a policy that became immortalized in the phrase *"40 acres and a mule."*  

The Gullah Geechee of the Sea Islands were among the first beneficiaries. By mid-1865, roughly **40,000 formerly enslaved people** had settled on these lands, establishing self-sufficient communities. However, President Andrew Johnson’s **pardons to former Confederates** in 1865 led to mass land repossessions, forcing many Black landowners into tenancy or sharecropping. Despite this betrayal, some Gullah Geechee families, particularly on St. Helena and Hilton Head Islands, successfully retained their land through legal battles and communal resistance.  

## **3. The Freedmen’s Bureau and Land Sales**  
The **Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands (Freedmen’s Bureau)**, established in 1865, was tasked with overseeing land disputes and labor contracts. Under **General Oliver O. Howard**, the Bureau attempted to mediate between freedpeople and returning white landowners.  

In 1863, the U.S. government had auctioned confiscated lands, but Gullah Geechee families lacked the capital to compete with Northern speculators. Some, however, pooled resources to purchase tracts collectively. For example:  
- **The 1865 Mitchelville Purchase** – Freedmen on Hilton Head bought land to establish one of the first self-governed Black towns.  
- **St. Helena Island** – Families secured deeds through the **Direct Tax Commission**, a rare instance where Black ownership was legally recognized.  

Despite these victories, systemic racism and federal indifference allowed most land to revert to white control.  

## **4. Gullah Geechee Resistance and Land Retention Strategies**  
Faced with violent reprisals and legal trickery, Gullah Geechee communities developed strategies to protect their land:  
- **Collective Ownership** – Families shared titles under informal agreements, preventing forced sales.  
- **Oral Land Transfers** – Due to distrust of legal systems, many passed land through verbal wills, a practice that later led to legal disputes ("heirs’ property" issues).  
- **Economic Independence** – Fishing, farming, and crafts allowed self-sufficiency, reducing reliance on white landowners.  

One poignant example is **Thomas B. Chaplin**, a formerly enslaved man on St. Helena, who testified before Congress in 1866 about white planters’ attempts to reclaim land by force. His defiance reflected broader Gullah Geechee resilience.  

## **5. The End of the Experiment and Its Aftermath**  
By 1868, most of the redistributed land had been seized or sold back to former slaveholders. The Freedmen’s Bureau, underfunded and overwhelmed, failed to enforce Black property rights. Yet, in remote Sea Island enclaves, Gullah Geechee families held on.  

The experiment’s legacy includes:  
- **Cultural Preservation** – Isolated from mainland pressures, Gullah Geechee communities maintained African traditions in food, language, and spirituality.  
- **Legal Precedents** – Early land claims set the stage for 20th-century civil rights battles over Black land loss.  
- **Modern Land Struggles** – Today, groups like the **Penn Center** and **Gullah Geechee Land & Legacy Project** fight to protect ancestral lands from development and gentrification.  

## **Conclusion**  
The Port Royal Experiment was both a beacon of hope and a cautionary tale. While systemic racism and federal abandonment undermined its promise, Gullah Geechee perseverance ensured that some lands remained in Black hands. Their resistance—through legal action, economic independence, and cultural solidarity—continues to inspire contemporary movements for land justice. The struggle for ownership, rooted in the Sea Islands, remains central to the Gullah Geechee story.
