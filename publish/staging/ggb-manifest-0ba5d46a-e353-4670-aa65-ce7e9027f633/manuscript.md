# **Gullah Geechee Midwifery and Birth Traditions**  

**Volume 42 of the Gullah Geechee Encyclopedia Series**  

## **Introduction**  

Gullah Geechee midwifery and birth traditions represent a profound legacy of African cultural retention, resilience, and community care. Rooted in the wisdom of West and Central African healers, Gullah Geechee midwives (*granny midwives* or *nana midwives*) served as pillars of health and spirituality in their communities. From the antebellum period through the mid-20th century, these women skillfully merged ancestral knowledge with locally available remedies to guide childbirth while maintaining African spiritual and medicinal practices.  

This chapter explores the historical foundations of Gullah Geechee midwifery, the spiritual and herbal practices employed, the role of midwives as cultural bearers, resistance to medical erasure, and contemporary revivals of these traditions. Centering Gullah Geechee voices, this study highlights the enduring significance of midwifery as an expression of autonomy, faith, and communal resilience.  

---  

## **1. Historical Foundations of Gullah Geechee Midwifery**  

### **African Roots and Transatlantic Continuities**  
Gullah Geechee midwifery finds its origins in West and Central African traditions. In societies such as the Yoruba, Igbo, Mende, and Kongo, midwives (*iyalorisa*, *dibia*, or *nganga*) held sacred roles as mediators between the physical and spiritual realms. African midwives were revered not only for their obstetric skills but also for their knowledge of medicinal herbs (*obeah* or *nzambi*) and rituals that ensured safe passage into motherhood.  

Enslaved African women brought these practices to the Sea Islands and coastal regions of South Carolina, Georgia, and North Florida. Despite colonial oppression, they preserved birth rituals, herbal remedies, and spiritual protections—practices often dismissed or suppressed by Eurocentric medicine.  

### **Midwifery During Slavery and Reconstruction**  
Under slavery, plantation owners relied on enslaved midwives to deliver both Black and white infants, a dual role that granted some midwives limited social mobility. Historical records, such as those from the Georgia Sea Islands, mention figures like Maum Katie, a midwife who facilitated numerous births on St. Helena Island.  

After emancipation, Gullah Geechee midwives remained central to rural communities. Unlike urban areas where white-dominated hospitals excluded Black women, the Sea Islands depended on these practitioners. The Freedmen’s Bureau notes midwives delivering babies when no physicians were available, reinforcing their indispensable role.

## **2. Spiritual and Herbal Traditions in Childbirth**  

### **Protective Rituals and Ancestral Invocations**  
Birth was not solely a medical event but a spiritual journey. Midwives employed prayers, chants, and grounding rituals to protect mother and child. Common practices included:  
- **Placenta Burial** – The placenta (*navel string*) was buried beneath a tree (often a cedar or oak) to spiritually tether the child to their homeland.  
- **Calling the Ancestors** – Midwives invoked ancestral spirits for protection, a tradition linked to West African cosmologies.  
- **Cord Charms** – Umbilical cords were sometimes placed in small pouches with protective herbs like *John the Conqueror root* to ward off evil.  

### **Herbal Remedies and Healing Practices**  
Gullah Geechee midwives relied on locally available plants, many with direct African parallels:  
- **Motherwort (*Leonurus cardiaca*)** – Used to ease labor pains (similar to its use among the Akan people).  
- **Cotton Root Bark (*Gossypium herbaceum*)** – A contraceptive and labor inducer, historically used by enslaved women to resist forced reproduction.  
- **Life Everlasting (*Pseudognaphalium obtusifolium*)** – Brewed into tea to soothe postpartum bleeding.  

These remedies were passed orally, with no formal medical training required—knowledge came from years of apprenticeship.

## **3. The Midwife as Cultural Bearer and Community Leader**  

### **Beyond Childbirth: The Social Role of Midwives**  
Midwives served as counselors, matchmakers, and funeral attendants. Their authority extended beyond the birthing room into all stages of life. In Beaufort, South Carolina, midwives like Hattie Knox were known to prepare burial garments and assist in *settin’ up* ceremonies (overnight vigils).  

### **Oral Histories and Knowledge Transmission**  
Unlike Western obstetrics, Gullah midwifery was taught experientially. Young apprentices learned by attending deliveries, gathering herbs, and observing elder midwives. Famous Gullah midwife **Maude Callen** (1898–1990) famously trained under her aunt before serving for decades in rural South Carolina, later documented by Life Magazine in 1951.  

### **Resisting Medical Marginalization**  
Despite their expertise, midwives faced increasing regulation in the early 20th century. The **Sheppard-Towner Act (1921)** and later licensing laws sought to replace traditional birth attendants with male-dominated obstetrics. Many Gullah midwives, however, continued practicing discreetly, maintaining trust within their communities.  

---  

## **4. Decline and Medical Erasure of Gullah Midwifery**  

### **The Push for Hospital Births**  
By the 1940s, medical authorities framed home births as “backward,” pressuring Black women into segregated hospitals with higher mortality rates. Midwives were required to secure permits, and states like South Carolina imposed strict regulations that pushed many out of practice.  

### **Legacy of Granny Midwives**  
Figures such as **Grandma Susie Smith** (of Sapelo Island) and **Aunt Hagar Brown** (Georgia) became living archives of knowledge. Their practices were documented by ethnographers like **Muriel Miller Wright**, ensuring their contributions were not erased.  

---  

## **5. Contemporary Revivals and Cultural Reclamation**  

### **Modern-Day Midwifery Movements**  
Today, Gullah Geechee descendants and Black midwifery advocates are reclaiming these traditions. Organizations like the **International Center for Traditional Childbearing** and **Sista Midwife Productions** emphasize culturally-rooted maternal care.  

### **Herbalism and Birth Justice**  
Initiatives such as the **Gullah Geechee Herbal Healing Project** teach ancestral plant medicine, while birth workers integrate spiritual rituals into modern doula practices.  

---  

## **Conclusion**  

Gullah Geechee midwifery is a testament to African cultural endurance. These traditions embody resistance, spirituality, and self-determination. While systemic forces sought to erase them, contemporary revivals prove their lasting significance. As birth justice movements grow, the wisdom of Gullah midwives remains a guiding light.
