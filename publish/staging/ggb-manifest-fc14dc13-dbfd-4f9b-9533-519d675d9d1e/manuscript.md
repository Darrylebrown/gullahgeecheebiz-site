# **The Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor**  
*Volume 07 of the Gullah Geechee Encyclopedia Series*  

## **Introduction**  
The Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor is a federally designated National Heritage Area established in 2006 to recognize, preserve, and celebrate the unique cultural contributions of the Gullah Geechee people. Spanning over 12,000 square miles along the Atlantic coast from North Carolina to Florida, the Corridor encompasses the Sea Islands and Lowcountry regions where Gullah Geechee communities have thrived for centuries.  

The Corridor was created through the efforts of grassroots activists, scholars, and elected officials who sought to protect the endangered African cultural retentions of the Gullah Geechee people—descendants of enslaved West and Central Africans who developed a distinct creole language, spiritual traditions, foodways, and artistic expressions. This chapter explores the Corridor’s historical foundations, significance, cultural preservation efforts, challenges, and enduring legacy.  

## **1. Historical Foundations of the Corridor**  

### **Enslavement and the Formation of Gullah Geechee Culture**  
The Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor’s origins are rooted in the transatlantic slave trade. Between the 17th and 19th centuries, thousands of Africans—primarily from the Windward Coast (Senegal to Liberia), the Gold Coast (Ghana), and the Bight of Benin (Togo, Benin, Nigeria)—were forcibly brought to the Southeastern U.S. The isolated Sea Islands, with their malaria-infested marshes, discouraged white settlement, allowing African traditions to persist with minimal interference.  

Enslaved laborers cultivated rice, indigo, and cotton using West African agricultural techniques, and their linguistic, spiritual, and culinary practices merged into a distinctive culture. The term "Gullah" (likely from the Gola people of Liberia) refers to descendants in the Carolinas and Georgia, while "Geechee" (possibly from the Kissi or Ogeechee River) is more common in Georgia and Florida.  

### **Post-Emancipation and Community Resilience**  
After emancipation in 1865, many Gullah Geechee families remained on the Sea Islands, maintaining their land through Reconstruction and beyond. The St. Helena Island community, for example, resisted land loss through mutual aid societies like the Penn School (founded 1862), one of the first schools for formerly enslaved Black Southerners. Despite pressures from tourism, development, and systemic land dispossession, Gullah Geechee communities preserved their ancestral traditions well into the 20th century.  

## **2. Establishment of the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor**  

### **Legislative Efforts and Recognition**  
In the 1990s and early 2000s, Gullah Geechee leaders like Marquetta Goodwine (Queen Quet, Chieftess of the Gullah Geechee Nation) and scholars such as Dr. Emory Campbell advocated for federal recognition of Gullah Geechee heritage. Their efforts culminated in the Gullah/Geechee Cultural Heritage Act, passed by Congress in 2006, which established the Corridor under the National Park Service (NPS).  

Unlike a national park, the Corridor operates as a partnership between local communities, nonprofits, and federal agencies. A 15-member federal commission, including Gullah Geechee representatives, guides its mission.  

### **Geographic Scope and Key Sites**  
The Corridor stretches from Pender County, North Carolina, to St. Johns County, Florida, including historic sites such as:  
- **Sapelo Island, GA** – Hog Hammock community, one of the last Geechee settlements.  
- **Penn Center, SC** – A historic educational and cultural preservation site.  
- **Kingsley Plantation, FL** – A former plantation highlighting African resistance.  
- **St. Simons Island, GA** – The site of Igbo Landing, where enslaved Africans staged a mass suicide in protest in 1803.  

## **3. Cultural Preservation and Revitalization**  

### **Language and Oral Traditions**  
The Gullah language, an English-based creole with West African grammatical structures, remains a cornerstone of identity. Scholars like Lorenzo Dow Turner (author of *Africanisms in the Gullah Dialect*, 1949) documented its survival. Organizations like the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Commission support language programs. Storytelling, folktales (e.g., the Br'er Rabbit tales), and spirituals also continue as living traditions.  

### **Craftsmanship and Visual Arts**  
Basket weaving (using sweetgrass, bulrush, and pine needles) is a revered skill passed down from enslaved ancestors. Renowned artisans like Mary Jackson (1945–2022) elevated this craft to national acclaim. The Corridor supports initiatives like the Sweetgrass Cultural Arts Festival in Mount Pleasant, SC.  

### **Culinary Heritage**  
Gullah Geechee cuisine—featuring okra soup, red rice, and benne wafers—retains direct African influences. Chefs like Benjamin "B.J." Dennis promote these traditions in modern culinary spaces.  

## **4. Contemporary Challenges and Advocacy**  

### **Land Dispossession and Development Pressures**  
Rapid coastal development threatens ancestral lands. The 1980s “Reconstruction Era” land grabs—such as on Hilton Head Island—displaced many families. Organizations like the Gullah/Geechee Sea Island Coalition fight for land rights.  

### **Climate Change and Environmental Justice**  
Rising sea levels and hurricanes endanger historic communities. The Gullah Geechee Climate Action Network, led by leaders like Queen Quet, advocates for sustainable policies.  

## **Conclusion**  
The Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor stands as a testament to resilience, safeguarding a living culture shaped by resistance and adaptation. While challenges persist, the Corridor ensures that future generations will continue to honor the legacy of their ancestors through education, advocacy, and cultural pride.
