# **Volume 38: The Role of the Church in Gullah Geechee Communities**  

## **Introduction**  

The church has long been the spiritual, cultural, and organizational cornerstone of Gullah Geechee communities, serving as far more than a place of worship. For generations, African-descended people in the Lowcountry regions of South Carolina, Georgia, Florida, and North Carolina have relied on the church as a sanctuary of resistance, resilience, and cultural preservation. Rooted in West African religious traditions and shaped by the harsh realities of slavery and segregation, Gullah Geechee Christianity developed unique practices—ring shouts, call-and-response preaching, and spirituals—that distinguish it from mainstream Protestantism.  

From the praise houses of the Sea Islands to the AME and Baptist congregations that became centers of civil rights organizing, the church has been instrumental in sustaining Gullah Geechee identity. This chapter explores the historical evolution of the church within these communities, its cultural significance, and its role in social and political movements.  

---  

## **1. Historical Foundations: From West African Spirituality to Lowcountry Christianity**  

The religious traditions of Gullah Geechee people are deeply informed by the spiritual practices of their West and Central African ancestors, including the Yoruba, Kongo, and Mende peoples. Enslaved Africans brought with them concepts of ancestor veneration, spirit possession, and communal worship, which were later syncretized with Protestant Christianity.  

Missionaries, particularly Baptists and Methodists, began converting enslaved people in the 18th century, but Gullah Geechee worship retained African-inflected practices. **Praise houses**, small, informal chapels on plantations, became spaces where enslaved people could gather secretly, blending Christian hymns with African rhythms and movements, giving rise to the **ring shout**—a sacred dance of spiritual ecstasy.  

After emancipation, formerly enslaved people established independent Black churches, such as **First African Baptist Church in Savannah (1773)** and **Bryan Neck Baptist Church in McIntosh County, Georgia (1865)**, which remain vital institutions today.  

---  

## **2. Worship and Ritual: The Distinctiveness of Gullah Geechee Spirituality**  

Gullah Geechee worship is characterized by its emotional intensity, participatory nature, and retention of African traditions. Key elements include:  

- **Call-and-Response Preaching**: Ministers engage the congregation in a dynamic exchange, a practice rooted in African oral traditions.  
- **Spirituals and Gospel Music**: Songs like "Kumbaya" (originating from Gullah "Come by Here") and "Nobody Knows the Trouble I’ve Seen" emerged from the Sea Islands.  
- **Ring Shouts**: A counterclockwise dance accompanied by rhythmic clapping and singing, believed to invoke the Holy Spirit.  

These practices were documented by folklorists like **Lydia Parrish** (*Slave Songs of the Georgia Sea Islands*, 1942) and **Alan Lomax**, who recorded Gullah spiritual performances in the 1930s.  

---  

## **3. The Church as a Community Institution: Education and Mutual Aid**  

Beyond spirituality, Gullah Geechee churches have historically served as centers for education and mutual aid. During Reconstruction, Black churches founded **freedmen’s schools**, such as **Penn School on St. Helena Island (1862)**, which later became a hub for preserving Gullah culture.  

Churches also organized **benevolent societies**, providing financial support for funerals, medical care, and disaster relief—a tradition continuing today through groups like the **Gullah Geechee Angel Network**.  

---  

## **4. Resistance and Liberation: The Church in the Civil Rights Movement**  

Gullah Geechee churches were critical to the Civil Rights Movement. **Reverend Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.** frequently visited the Sea Islands, drawing inspiration from Gullah spirituals for sermons. Locally, leaders like **Esau Jenkins** (founder of the **Citizenship Schools**) used church networks to teach literacy and voter registration, combating Jim Crow laws.  

The **Beaufort County Black Chamber of Commerce** and **NAACP** chapters often met in churches, which provided safe spaces for organizing protests, such as the 1969 **Hospital Workers’ Strike in Charleston**.  

---  

## **5. Cultural Preservation: The Church in Modern Gullah Geechee Identity**  

Today, churches remain central to preserving Gullah Geechee heritage. Annual events like the **Gullah Geechee Homecoming Celebrations** feature worship services with traditional music and storytelling. Congregations such as **Brick Baptist Church on St. Helena Island (1855)** and **Campbell AME Church in Charleston** actively promote heritage tourism and language preservation.  

However, challenges such as **gentrification, climate change, and declining membership** threaten these institutions. Organizations like the **Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor Commission** work to protect historic churches as cultural landmarks.  

---  

## **Conclusion**  

The church has been—and continues to be—the heartbeat of Gullah Geechee life, intertwining faith, culture, and resistance. From praise houses to political organizing, these sacred spaces have nurtured a distinct African American spiritual tradition while adapting to modern challenges. As Gullah Geechee communities fight to sustain their heritage, the church remains a vital force in ensuring their survival.
