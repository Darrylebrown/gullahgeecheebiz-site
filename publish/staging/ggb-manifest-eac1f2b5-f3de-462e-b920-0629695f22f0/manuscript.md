## Chapter 48.1: The 1989 Gullah Homecoming

Fourteen Gullah delegates visited Sierra Leone for a week of national celebration, led by Emory Campbell, Director of Penn Center.

### Key Moments
- President Joseph Saidu Momoh addressed the delegation in Krio
- "A.K. Turay, the president's chief of staff, read a proclamation calling upon the 'common ancestors of Sierra Leoneans and Gullahs' to bless the homecoming"
- Emory Campbell was "anointed on his forehead with the soil of Bunce Island"
- The delegation visited Freetown, Bunce Island, and Bo

### Media Documentation
- *Family Across the Sea* (1990, SCETV)
- Newspaper coverage in both Sierra Leone and the United States

## Chapter 48.2: The 1997 Moran Family Homecoming

Mary Moran visited the Mende village where her family's funeral song is still sung. The journey was documented in *The Language You Cry In* (California Newsreel, 1998).

### Wilson Moran's Reflection
Her son Wilson stood in the Bunce Island slave yard and said: "I feel a lot of pain here, a lot of pain... what man can do to man."

### The Song Connection
The Mende funeral song, preserved by the Moran family of Harris Neck, Georgia, for over 200 years, was confirmed as identical to a song still sung in a remote Mende village — the only documented case of an African language text preserved intact across the Atlantic.

## Chapter 48.3: The 2005 Priscilla Homecoming

Thomalind Polite's journey, documented through the extraordinary paper trail connecting her to a 10-year-old girl on the *Hare* in 1756.

### The Journey
- Departed Charleston for Freetown
- Visited Bunce Island and walked the slave yard
- Met with government officials and cultural leaders
- Returned to Charleston with soil from Sierra Leone

---

## Chapter 48.4: Isaiah Washington's DNA Journey

In 2006, actor Isaiah Washington visited Bunce Island after learning through DNA testing that he was descended from the Mende people. He later donated $25,000 for a computer reconstruction of the castle.

---

## Chapter 48.5: The Significance of Homecomings

These journeys represent more than tourism or personal discovery. They are acts of restorative justice — reclaiming connections that the slave trade sought to destroy. As the Sierra Leonean cabinet minister said at the 1989 homecoming: Bunce Island is "a little piece of Africa that was destroyed to build America."
