# **The Penn School and Education in the Sea Islands**  

## **Introduction**  

The Penn School, founded in 1862 on St. Helena Island, South Carolina, stands as one of the first schools in the United States established to educate formerly enslaved African Americans in the Sea Islands. Emerging in the wake of the Port Royal Experiment—a Union-led initiative to transition the region from slavery to free labor—the school became a cornerstone of Gullah Geechee education, self-determination, and cultural preservation.  

Rooted in the values of abolitionist philanthropy and African American agency, the Penn School provided academic instruction, agricultural training, and vocational skills while affirming Gullah Geechee heritage. Over its 86-year history (1862–1948), the institution evolved from a missionary endeavor into a community-driven force, deeply influencing civil rights and African American education. This chapter explores the school’s origins, its impact on Sea Islands education, and its enduring legacy within the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor.  

---

## **1. The Founding of the Penn School: Abolitionism, War, and Freedom**  

The Penn School was established during the Civil War, shortly after Union forces occupied the South Carolina Sea Islands in November 1861. Enslaved Gullah Geechee people, left behind by fleeing Confederate planters, were declared "contraband of war" and placed under Northern administration. Recognizing the need for education, two abolitionist missionaries—Laura M. Towne and Ellen Murray—founded the school in April 1862 with support from the Freedmen’s Aid Society.  

Named after the abolitionist William Penn, the school initially operated out of the abandoned plantation of John Joyner Smith. By 1865, it had grown into a formal institution under the auspices of the Pennsylvania Freedmen’s Relief Association. Early instruction focused on literacy, arithmetic, and religious studies, with Gullah Geechee adults and children alike attending classes. Notably, the school avoided the paternalistic tendencies of many Northern-led initiatives by incorporating African American teachers, including Charlotte Forten, a prominent Black educator and poet.  

---

## **2. Curriculum and Community Impact: From Literacy to Landownership**  

Unlike other Freedmen’s schools that prioritized industrial training, the Penn School balanced classical education with practical skills tailored to Sea Islands life. Students studied:  

- **Literacy and Mathematics**: Core academic subjects were taught to foster self-sufficiency and civic participation.  
- **Agriculture and Trades**: Carpentry, sewing, and farming (particularly cotton and sweet potatoes) were emphasized, reflecting local economic needs.  
- **Cultural Heritage**: Oral storytelling, hymns, and folklore were integrated into learning, resisting forced assimilation.  

Crucially, the school supported landownership. Many Gullah Geechee families, through the school’s advocacy, secured land under the 1865 Special Field Orders No. 15 (the "40 acres and a mule" promise). This fostered generational stability, keeping many Black-owned farms intact even after Reconstruction’s failures.  

---

## **3. Transition to the Penn Normal, Industrial, and Agricultural School (1901–1948)**  

By the early 20th century, the Penn School adapted to shifting educational philosophies. Under principal Rossa B. Cooley (served 1901–1939) and co-principal Grace B. House, the institution became the **Penn Normal, Industrial, and Agricultural School**, aligning with Booker T. Washington’s Tuskegee Model. Key changes included:  

- **Teacher Training**: The school prepared Gullah Geechee educators, ensuring locally rooted instruction.  
- **Extension Programs**: Home economics and public health initiatives addressed community needs.  
- **Preservation Efforts**: Anthropologists like Lorenzo Dow Turner documented Gullah language and traditions with school support.  

Despite funding challenges during the Great Depression, the school remained a vital hub until its closure in 1948, when public schools began integrating Black students.  

---

## **4. The Penn Center: Civil Rights and Cultural Preservation (1950s–Present)**  

After closing as a school, the campus became the **Penn Center**, a retreat and meeting space for civil rights leaders. Notably:  

- **Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.** drafted portions of his "I Have a Dream" speech here in 1962.  
- **Septima Clark**, a South Carolina educator and activist, conducted citizenship schools at Penn to combat voter suppression.  

Today, the Penn Center is a **National Historic Landmark** and a pillar of Gullah Geechee heritage, offering archives, cultural workshops, and land retention programs.  

---

## **5. Education in the Sea Islands: Broader Implications**  

The Penn School set a precedent for African American education in the region, influencing:  

- **The Beaufort County School System**, which later absorbed Penn’s students.  
- **Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs)**, including nearby Allen University.  
- **Cultural Literacy Programs**, such as the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor’s initiatives.  

Its legacy underscores education as both liberation and cultural affirmation in the Gullah Geechee community.  

---

## **Conclusion**  

The Penn School represents more than an educational institution; it embodies the resilience of the Sea Islands’ African-descended people. From abolitionist beginnings to civil rights activism, it nurtured generations of Gullah Geechee leaders, landowners, and scholars. Today, the Penn Center continues this mission, preserving a vital chapter in American history while centering the voices of those it served.
