# **Sea Island Folklore and Supernatural Beliefs**  
**Volume 45 of the Gullah Geechee Encyclopedia Series**  

## **Introduction**  
The Sea Islands—stretching from North Carolina to Florida—are home to the Gullah Geechee people, descendants of enslaved West and Central Africans who preserved their ancestral traditions despite centuries of oppression. Among the most enduring aspects of Gullah Geechee culture is its rich folklore, deeply entwined with spiritual beliefs, supernatural phenomena, and communal wisdom. These traditions reflect a syncretism of African cosmologies, Christian influences, and indigenous knowledge, forming a unique worldview where the physical and spiritual realms are inseparable.  

Supernatural beliefs in Gullah Geechee culture are not mere superstitions but deeply held philosophies that guide daily life, healing practices, and moral lessons. Rooted in the traumas of slavery and survival, these narratives often feature trickster figures, ancestral spirits, and omens, reinforcing values of resistance, unity, and resilience. This chapter explores the folklore and supernatural traditions of the Sea Islands, examining their origins, key figures, rituals, and contemporary relevance.

---

## **1. African Roots and Syncretism in Gullah Geechee Beliefs**  

### **Retentions from West and Central Africa**  
Gullah Geechee supernatural traditions trace their origins to the spiritual systems of the Kongo, Yoruba, Igbo, Mende, and other African ethnic groups. Enslaved Africans brought with them beliefs in:  
- **Ancestral veneration** – The dead remain present and influential in the lives of the living.  
- **Spirit possession and mediumship** – Certain individuals could communicate with spirits (later evolving into "root doctors" or "spiritual mothers").  
- **Cosmic duality** – The coexistence of the seen and unseen worlds, reflected in proverbs like *"Ef yuh cyaan see um, um still deh ya."* (If you can’t see it, it’s still here.)  

### **Christian Influences and Adaptations**  
Missionary efforts during slavery sought to eradicate African spirituality, but Gullah Geechee people reinterpreted Christian figures to align with traditional beliefs:  
- **Jesus as a conjure man** – Described as a healer who used herbs and divine power.  
- **Biblical figures as tricksters** – Moses, for example, was likened to African trickster deities who outsmarted oppressors.  

The fusion of African and Christian beliefs created a distinctive cosmology where protective charms, Bible verses, and ritual practices coexist.

## **2. Supernatural Figures in Gullah Folklore**  

### **The Boo Hag**  
One of the most feared entities, the **Boo Hag**, is a skinless, vampiric witch who steals breath (energy) at night. To ward her off:  
- **Blue paint around doors/windows** – Derived from the Kongo tradition of *nkisi* markers.  
- **Salt or rice grains** – Scattered to force the hag to count them until sunrise.  

Similar to the **Soucouyant** of the Caribbean, the Boo Hag embodies fears of nocturnal invasion and unseen threats, possibly symbolizing slave patrols or overseers in earlier oral traditions.  

### **Plateye (Plat-Eye)**  
A shape-shifting, one-legged demon that appears as a grotesque animal (often a dog or goat), the *Plateye* lures victims into marshes. Its origins may lie in the Ghanaian *Sasabonsam* or the Kongo *mbulu*. Folk remedies against it include:  
- Crossing a broomstick over a doorway.  
- Reciting Psalm 23 for protection.  

### **The Rolling Calf**  
A fiery-eyed bull (sometimes a ghostly mule) that chases sinners at night, linked to Jamaican *Rolling Calf* lore. Gullah storytellers caution that encountering one means you have committed a wrong that must be atoned for.  

### **Root Doctors and Conjurers**  
Known as *"trabblers"* or *"two-heads,"* these spiritual practitioners mediate between the living and the dead. Famous examples:  
- **Dr. Buzzard** (Dr. James Edwin McTeer) – A legendary Beaufort root doctor.  
- **Aunt Sarah** – A midwife and healer from St. Helena Island.  

Their work involved:  
- **Hoodoo (rootwork)** – Protective charms, love spells.  
- **Divination** – Reading bones, shells, or cards.

## **3. The Power of Signs, Omens, and Dreams**  

### **Death Omens**  
- **A bird entering the house** – Signifies impending death.  
- **The "death watch"** – A phantom knock (three raps) foretells someone’s passing.  
- **The "shadow man"** – Seeing one’s own shadow without a head is an omen of death.  

### **Dream Interpretation**  
- **Dreaming of teeth falling out** – Warns of family conflict.  
- **Dreaming of water** – Indicates spiritual cleansing or upcoming change.  
- **Dreaming of the dead speaking** – A direct message from ancestors, often requiring action.

## **4. Protective Rituals and Folk Magic**  

### **Haint Blue**  
Derived from indigo dye traditions, pale blue paint on porch ceilings and shutters repels *haints* (restless spirits). The practice comes from the Kongo belief in *nkisi* (spirit barriers).  

### **Mojo Bags & Jack Balls**  
Small cloth bundles filled with roots, herbs, and personal items (iron nails, graveyard dirt) carried for protection. Example ingredients:  
- **John the Conqueror root** – For strength.  
- **Devil’s Shoestring** – To trip evil spirits.  

### **Graveyard Traditions**  
- Turning clothes inside out before passing a cemetery to confuse spirits.  
- Pouring libations (water, whiskey) to honor ancestors.

## **5. The Role of Folktales in Moral Instruction**  

### **Br'er Rabbit Stories**  
Borrowed from Bantu trickster tales, **Br’er Rabbit** outsmarts stronger foes (Br’er Fox, Br’er Bear) through wit, symbolizing resistance to oppression.  

### **The Duppy (Ghost) Stories**  
Tales of vengeful spirits—often wronged in life—teach lessons about justice and karmic retribution.

## **6. Contemporary Revival and Challenges**  

### **Preservation Efforts**  
- **The Penn Center** (St. Helena Island) archives oral histories.  
- **Modern rootworkers** continue practices, adapting to urban settings.  

### **Misappropriation and Commercialization**  
- Hoodoo commodified in pop culture (e.g., "love spells" sold online).  
- Academic recognition remains limited despite UNESCO’s Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor designation.  

---

## **Conclusion**  
The supernatural beliefs of the Sea Islands are living traditions, evolving yet deeply rooted in ancestral wisdom. Far from mere superstition, they encode histories of survival, resistance, and spirituality. Recognizing their value ensures the continuity of Gullah Geechee heritage for future generations.
