# **Gullah Geechee Coastal Ecology and Stewardship**  
**Volume 39 of the Gullah Geechee Encyclopedia Series**  

## **Introduction**  
The Gullah Geechee people, descendants of enslaved Africans from the Lowcountry regions of South Carolina, Georgia, Florida, and North Carolina, have maintained a profound symbiotic relationship with their coastal environment for over 300 years. Their unparalleled ecological knowledge, shaped by West and Central African traditions, has fostered sustainable land and marine stewardship practices that continue to inform contemporary conservation efforts.  

Despite displacement from land dispossession, overdevelopment, and climate change, the Gullah Geechee have safeguarded critical ecosystems—barrier islands, salt marshes, maritime forests, and estuaries—through intergenerational wisdom and advocacy. This chapter examines Gullah Geechee coastal ecology, the threats to their environment, and their enduring leadership in ecological preservation.  

---  

## **1. The Gullah Geechee Coast: A Unique Biocultural Landscape**  

### **1.1 Geographic and Ecological Features**  
The Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor, designated by Congress in 2006, spans 12,000 square miles of coastal and island ecosystems. These areas include:  
- **Barrier Islands (e.g., Sapelo Island, GA; St. Helena Island, SC)** – Sheltered former plantation lands with rich, biodiverse habitats.  
- **Salt Marshes** – Often called "the nurseries of the sea," these wetlands purify water and buffer storms.  
- **Maritime Forests** – Live oaks, palmettos, and pines adapted to salt spray, crucial for preventing erosion.  
- **Estuaries and Tidal Creeks** – Vital for fishing, shrimping, and oyster harvesting.  

### **1.2 African Ecological Continuities**  
Gullah Geechee land management reflects African agroecological practices:  
- **Tidal Rice Cultivation** – Derived from West African (e.g., Sierra Leonean) techniques, using controlled flooding for nutrient distribution.  
- **Polyculture Farming** – Planting complementary crops (okra, black-eyed peas, benne) in the same field, minimizing soil depletion.  
- **Sacred Groves** – Preserved forest patches for spiritual and medicinal use, akin to West African *ygiri* tradition.

## **2. Traditional Stewardship Practices**  

### **2.1 Subsistence Fishing and Sustainable Harvesting**  
Gullah Geechee fishing practices emphasize balance:  
- **Seine Net Fishing** – Allowed small-scale, selective harvest without depleting stocks.  
- **Oyster Bed Rotation** – Harvesting alternate patches ensures regeneration, an Indigenous practice later validated by marine science.  
- **Moon and Tide Knowledge** – Elders like **Cornelia Bailey (Sapelo Island, GA)** documented lunar cycles dictating optimal fishing times.  

### **2.2 Land Preservation Through Heir Property**  
Many Gullah Geechee families hold land via **"heir property"**—a communal inheritance system. Though legally vulnerable (leading to forced sales), this tradition inherently prevents overdevelopment. Organizations like the **Penn Center (St. Helena Island, SC)** assist in legal protection.  

### **2.3 Fire Management and Controlled Burning**  
Adapted from African and Indigenous practices:  
- **"Burnin’ the Woods"** – Controlled burns to replenish soil and prevent destructive wildfires.  
- **Sweetgrass Basket Harvesting** – Burns maintain meadows where sweetgrass (*Muhlenbergia sericea*) thrives for Gullah artisans.

## **3. Threats to Gullah Geechee Coastal Ecology**  

### **3.1 Land Dispossession and Development Pressures**  
- **1970s–Present: Resort and Golf Course Expansion** – Hilton Head Island (SC) and Sea Island (GA) developments displaced historic Gullah communities.  
- **"Tax Land Grabs"** – Rising property taxes on heir lands force sales, as seen in **Daufuskie Island (SC)**.  

### **3.2 Climate Change Impacts**  
- **Sea Level Rise** – Parishes like **Christ Church (SC)** lose 2–5 feet of land annually.  
- **Increased Hurricanes** – Storms like **Hurricane Matthew (2016)** devastated Gullah farms and cemeteries.  

### **3.3 Industrial Pollution and Overfishing**  
- **1930s–1950s: Paper Mill Contamination (Savannah River)** – Destroyed fish stocks, harming Gullah fishermen.  
- **Modern Shrimp Trawling** – Industrial bycatch disrupts traditional shrimping.

## **4. Gullah Geechee-led Conservation Movements**  

### **4.1 Advocacy and Policy Influence**  
- **Queen Quet (Marquetta Goodwine)** – Chieftess of the Gullah/Geechee Nation, lobbied for federal protections, including the **Gullah/Geechee Fishing Association’s** sustainable fishing initiatives.  
- **The ACE Basin Project (SC)** – Collaborative conservation between Gullah families and NGOs to protect 350,000 acres.  

### **4.2 Cultural Tourism as Preservation**  
- **Gullah Geechee Heritage Tours (Sallie Ann Robinson, Daufuskie Island)** – Educates tourists on sustainable ecology.  
- **The Slave Dwelling Project (Joseph McGill, SC)** – Highlights historic land stewardship.  

---  

## **5. Contemporary Challenges and Innovations**  

### **5.1 Legal and Educational Efforts**  
- **Pro Bono Heirs’ Property Clinics** – Universities (e.g., USC Law School) aid in land retention.  
- **Gullah Geechee STEM Programs** – Teaching youth African-descended agroecology.  

### **5.2 Climate Resilience Strategies**  
- **Living Shorelines** – Using oyster reefs and marsh grass to combat erosion.  
- **Sacred Cemetery Relocation** – Moving burial sites inland with ancestral rites.  

---  

## **Conclusion**  
The Gullah Geechee relationship with their coastal environment is a testament to resilience and ecological wisdom. Their stewardship—grounded in African traditions, adaptive innovation, and activism—offers a blueprint for sustainable coexistence. Yet, systemic inequities persist, demanding urgent support for land sovereignty and climate justice.
