# **Gullah Geechee Dance and Movement Traditions**  

## **Introduction**  

The Gullah Geechee people, descendants of enslaved Africans in the coastal regions of the southeastern United States (from North Carolina to Florida), have preserved a vibrant cultural heritage rooted in West and Central African traditions. Among their most expressive and enduring practices are their dance and movement traditions, which serve as forms of communal healing, storytelling, spiritual connection, and resistance.  

Gullah Geechee dance is deeply intertwined with music, particularly ring shouts, work songs, and later, blues and gospel influences. These movements are not merely performances but living traditions that embody ancestral memory, cultural continuity, and identity. From the rhythmic footwork of the ring shout to the improvisational steps in harvest celebrations, Gullah Geechee dance reflects resilience, spirituality, and the unbroken thread of African diasporic expression.  

This chapter explores the historical roots, key forms, social functions, and contemporary adaptations of Gullah Geechee dance and movement traditions.  

---  

## **1. Historical Roots: African Retentions in Gullah Geechee Movement**  

Gullah Geechee dance traditions are strongly linked to West African and Central African cultural practices. Enslaved Africans brought with them diverse movement vocabularies, including:  

- **Circular/Ring Dances:** Found in many West African cultures, circle dances emphasize community and collective energy. The Gullah Geechee ring shout is a direct descendant of these traditions.  
- **Polyrhythmic Footwork:** Complex syncopated steps mirror African drumming traditions, particularly from societies like the Yoruba, Bakongo, and Mande.  
- **Possession and Spirit Movement:** Some dances were tied to spiritual possession rituals, akin to Yoruba **Orisha** dances or Kongo **nkisi** traditions.  

Despite enslavers' bans on drums and communal gatherings, Gullah Geechee people adapted, using body percussion (e.g., pattin’ juba) and call-and-response singing to maintain these traditions.  

---  

## **2. The Ring Shout: Spiritual Movement and Survival**  

The **ring shout** is the most iconic Gullah Geechee dance form, originating in West Africa and adapted in slave quarters and praise houses. Unlike European "shouting," this is a counterclockwise, rhythmic movement with participants singing, clapping, and shuffling feet in sync.  

### **Key Characteristics:**  
- **Counterclockwise Motion:** Reflects African cosmological beliefs (e.g., the Bakongo **"circle of life"**).  
- **Call-and-Response Singing:** Leaders (often elders) chant phrases echoed by the group.  
- **"Trance" States:** Some participants enter heightened spiritual states.  

Noted folklorist **Lorenzo Dow Turner** documented ring shouts in the 1930s, showing their persistence long after slavery. Today, groups like the **Geechee Gullah Ring Shouters** continue this sacred tradition.  

---  

## **3. Work Songs and Movement: Labor as Dance**  

Enslaved Africans transformed labor into collective rhythm. Movements in planting, harvesting, and even rowing were synchronized with song and movement traditions, such as:  

- **Rice Dance (Juba):** Mimicked the motions of rice pounding and sweeping.  
- **Rowin’ Songs:** Syncopated rowing movements on plantations near waterways.  
- **"Step-Down" Dances:** Celebratory dances after completing harvests.  

The **"Juba" (Hambone)** dance, involving patting the body to create rhythm, emerged from bans on drumming while maintaining musical expression.  

---  

## **4. Social and Funerary Dances: Celebrations and Commemorations**  

Gullah Geechee life-cycle events incorporated dance:  

- **"Jumping the Broom" Weddings:** Couples danced in a circle, symbolizing community unity.  
- **Funerary Dances:** Processional dances (like the **"Second Line"** influence) honored ancestors, ensuring their peaceful transition.  

The **"Buzzard Lope"** mimicked bird movements in funeral rites, symbolizing the spirit’s flight—similar to the Yoruba **Egungun** masquerades.  

---  

## **5. Contemporary Expressions: From Folk Revival to Hip-Hop**  

Gullah Geechee dance evolves while staying rooted:  

- **Dr. Vertamae Grosvenor’s** work in the 1960s–70s documented **"Gullah Kinfolk"** performances.  
- **Hidden Legacies in Black Dance:** Gullah Geechee footwork appears in tap, Charleston, and hip-hop (e.g., Atlanta’s "Snapping" dances).  
- **Festivals & Revivals:**  
  - **Penn Center’s Heritage Days**  
  - **Moja Arts Festival (Charleston, SC)**  

Modern artists, like **The McIntosh County Shouters**, keep tradition alive while younger generations blend it with new forms.  

---  

## **Conclusion: Movement as Memory**  

Gullah Geechee dance and movement traditions are more than entertainment—they are **embodied archives** of resistance, spirituality, and survival. From the sacred ring shout to celebratory juba dances, these forms have resisted erasure while continually adapting.  

As scholar **Ras Michael Brown** notes, Gullah Geechee movement is "a testament to the unbroken line from Africa to the Sea Islands." By sustaining these traditions, Gullah Geechee communities ensure that the past remains a living force in the present.
