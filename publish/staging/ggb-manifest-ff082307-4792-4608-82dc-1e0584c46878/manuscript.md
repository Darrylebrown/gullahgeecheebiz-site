# **Gullah Geechee Beadwork and Adornment**  
**Volume 48 of the *Gullah Geechee Encyclopedia Series***  

## **Introduction**  
Gullah Geechee beadwork and adornment represent a vibrant fusion of African heritage, cultural resilience, and artistic innovation. Originating from the enslaved West and Central Africans brought to the coastal regions of South Carolina, Georgia, North Carolina, and Florida, Gullah Geechee beadwork is more than decorative—it is a language of resistance, identity, and ancestral memory.  

This chapter explores the historical significance, techniques, materials, and symbolic meanings of Gullah Geechee beadwork and adornment. Drawing from oral histories, archaeological findings, and contemporary practices, we highlight how this tradition has been preserved and adapted across centuries.  

## **1. Historical Roots and African Continuities**  
### **1.1 African Origins and Transatlantic Exchange**  
The beadwork traditions of the Gullah Geechee people are deeply rooted in West African societies, particularly those of the Akan, Yoruba, Mende, and Bakongo peoples. In these cultures, beads were not merely ornamental but held spiritual, social, and economic significance.  

- **Trade Influence**: European colonizers introduced glass beads through the transatlantic trade, which enslaved Africans incorporated into existing traditions.  
- **Archaeological Evidence**: Excavations at sites like the **Stono Rebellion memorial (1739, South Carolina)** reveal cobalt-blue beads similar to those used in West African spiritual practices.  

### **1.2 Beads as Currency and Status Symbols**  
During slavery, beads were sometimes used as **alternative currency**, traded among enslaved people when money was forbidden. Certain colors and patterns indicated social roles:  
- **Red beads** symbolized protection (linked to Yoruba *ileke* tradition).  
- **Blue beads** (often called "witch beads") warded off evil spirits (a practice documented in *Drums and Shadows*, WPA interviews, 1940).  

## **2. Techniques and Materials in Traditional Gullah Geechee Beadwork**  
### **2.1 Handmade Bead Production**  
Early Gullah Geechee artisans crafted beads from:  
- **Seeds and nuts** (e.g., Job’s tears seeds, *Coix lacryma-jobi*).  
- **Bone, shell, and clay**—archaeological digs on Sapelo Island, Georgia, reveal pre-1860 clay bead workshops.  

### **2.2 European Trade Beads and Adaptation**  
After European contact, glass beads (like Venetian *cheveron* beads) became prominent. The Gullah Geechee repurposed them for:  
- **Necklaces ("money beads")**—worn as hidden savings (Oral histories from Edingsville, SC, 1930s).  
- **Waistbeads**—a West African tradition maintained for spiritual and health purposes.  

## **3. Symbolism and Spiritual Significance**  
### **3.1 Protective Adornment**  
Beads served as spiritual armor:  
- **Haint blue beads**: Worn to repel malevolent spirits (linked to Igbo *nkisi* traditions).  
- **Conjure bags (mojo bags)**: Contained beads alongside roots/herbs for protection.  

### **3.2 Rites of Passage and Communal Identity**  
- **First Bead Ceremonies**: Similar to Akan naming rites, infants received **white beads** for purity.  
- **Bridal waistbeads**: Adorned with **amber and coral** beads to ensure fertility (documented in Beaufort, SC, 1920s).  

## **4. Beadwork in Gullah Geechee Folklore and Oral Tradition**  
### **4.1 Stories of the "Talking Beads"**  
- Oral histories from **Mose Brown (St. Helena Island, 1912)** recount elders passing messages through bead patterns.  
- **The Legend of Sapphia’s Necklace**: A tale of escaped enslaved women using beaded codes to navigate the Underground Railroad.  

### **4.2 Proverbs and Sayings**  
- **"Dem bead ain’t jus’ pretty, dem buy freedom"**—old Gullah saying emphasizing bead value.  

## **5. Decline and Revival in the 20th–21st Centuries**  
### **5.1 The Great Migration and Cultural Erosion**  
Urbanization and assimilation pressures led to declining traditional crafts. By the **1950s**, synthetic plastics replaced handmade beads.  

### **5.2 Contemporary Revitalization**  
Artists like **Queen Quet (Chieftess of the Gullah Geechee Nation)** and the **Sweetgrass Cultural Arts Festival (Charleston, SC)** have reignited beadwork traditions:  
- **Modern Interpretations**: Artists such as **Sharon Cooper-Murray** incorporate recycled glass into traditional designs.  

## **Conclusion**  
Gullah Geechee beadwork is a testament to cultural endurance, blending African spirituality, resistance, and artistic brilliance. From protective charms to coded messages of freedom, beads have been silent witnesses to Gullah Geechee history. Today, revitalization efforts ensure these traditions continue to thrive.
