# **The Gullah Geechee Relationship with the Natural World**  

## **Introduction**  

The Gullah Geechee people, descendants of enslaved Africans who have resided in the coastal regions of South Carolina, Georgia, Florida, and North Carolina for centuries, maintain a profound and intricate relationship with the natural world. This bond is deeply rooted in West and Central African traditions, adaptive ecological knowledge, and a spiritual connection to the land and water. The Gullah Geechee’s subsistence practices, folklore, medicinal knowledge, and environmental stewardship reflect an enduring interdependence with their surroundings—one that has sustained their communities through slavery, Reconstruction, and into the present day.  

Their ecological wisdom, often dismissed or overlooked by outsiders, encompasses sustainable farming, fishing, and land management practices that have preserved the biodiversity of the Sea Islands and Lowcountry regions. This chapter explores the historical, cultural, and spiritual dimensions of the Gullah Geechee relationship with nature, demonstrating how their traditions continue to inform environmental resilience and cultural identity.  

## **1. African Continuities: Ecological Knowledge and Adaptation**  

The Gullah Geechee’s environmental practices are deeply informed by African agricultural and ecological traditions. Enslaved Africans brought with them expertise in tropical farming, such as rice cultivation, which became central to the Lowcountry economy. The knowledge of tidal irrigation and the use of African rice (*Oryza glaberrima*)—later hybridized with Asian varieties—helped establish South Carolina as a leading rice producer by the 18th century.  

Historical records, including letters from plantation owners like **Eliza Lucas Pinckney**, acknowledge that enslaved Africans were the true agricultural experts. The Gullah Geechee applied techniques such as:  
- **Basket weaving for winnowing rice** (using coiled sweetgrass baskets, a skill derived from West Africa).  
- **Controlled seasonal burns** to clear fields and replenish soil, a practice now recognized as beneficial for preventing wildfires.  

These African survivals illustrate the Gullah Geechee’s role as early environmental custodians.  

## **2. Land and Livelihood: Subsistence Farming and the Sea**  

Even after emancipation, Gullah Geechee communities maintained **sustainable subsistence farming** and **fishing traditions**, ensuring food sovereignty. Families cultivated:  
- **Okra, collards, and benne (sesame)**—staples with African origins.  
- **Sweet potatoes and peanuts**, drought-resistant crops crucial for survival.  

Fishing—particularly **cast-net throwing** and **seining**—relied on intimate knowledge of tidal patterns. Elders passed down "**fish stories**," oral accounts of optimal fishing times based on lunar cycles. The Gullah Geechee diet, rich in **blue crabs, oysters, and shrimp**, reflected a reciprocal relationship with marine ecosystems.  

However, industrial shrimp trawling and coastal development disrupted these lifeways—a theme modern activists like **Queen Quet**, Chieftess of the Gullah Geechee Nation, continue to oppose.  

## **3. The Spiritual Landscape: Nature in Gullah Geechee Cosmology**  

Nature is not merely a resource but a sacred presence in Gullah Geechee spirituality. Concepts from African traditional religions, such as:  
- **Hags and hauntings** tied to waterways and live oaks.  
- **Rootwork (conjure medicine)**, where herbs like **life everlasting** and **devil’s shoestring** healed physical and spiritual ailments.  

Hoodoo practices, a syncretic blend of African spirituality and Christianity, invoked natural elements—**red brick dust for protection, Spanish moss for dream work**. The sea, a symbolic site of ancestral trauma (the Middle Passage), also serves as a spiritual conduit—evident in "**ring shouts**" and baptisms performed in rivers.  

## **4. Environmental Threats and Resistance**  

Historically, the Gullah Geechee faced land dispossession through:  
- **The Dawes Act (1887)**, which privatized communal lands.  
- **20th-century eminent domain seizures**, like those for Hilton Head resorts.  

Today, **climate change** (rising sea levels) and unchecked development threaten sacred sites like **Daufuskie Island** and **St. Helena**. Organizations like the **Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor Commission** and the **Penn Center** advocate for land retention policies informed by indigenous ecological knowledge.  

## **5. Cultural Preservation and Ecological Justice**  

Efforts to document and protect Gullah Geechee environmental heritage include:  
- **Sweetgrass basket-making** (a UNESCO-recognized craft).  
- **Oral history projects** preserving agricultural and maritime knowledge.  

Activists emphasize that ecological justice for the Gullah Geechee demands **sovereignty over traditional lands** and inclusion in climate adaptation planning.  

## **Conclusion**  

The Gullah Geechee’s relationship with the natural world is a testament to resilience, cultural continuity, and sustainable coexistence. Their ecological practices—forged in resistance and necessity—offer models for biodiversity conservation today. Recognizing their contributions is not merely an academic exercise but an urgent call for environmental and reparative justice.
