# **Gullah Geechee Wood Carving and Craftsmanship**  
**Volume 44 of the Gullah Geechee Encyclopedia Series**  

## **Introduction**  
The Gullah Geechee people, descendants of enslaved Africans from the coastal regions of South Carolina, Georgia, Florida, and North Carolina, have preserved a vibrant cultural heritage through language, foodways, spirituality, and craftsmanship. Among their most enduring art forms is wood carving, a tradition rooted in West African aesthetics, spiritual beliefs, and practical utility. Gullah Geechee wood carvings range from functional objects like mortars and pestles to ceremonial staffs and intricate figurative sculptures. This chapter explores the history, techniques, symbolism, and contemporary significance of Gullah Geechee wood carving, emphasizing the voices of master carvers and cultural custodians.  

## **1. Historical Roots: African Continuities and Adaptations**  

### **West African Precedents**  
Gullah Geechee wood carving is deeply connected to African traditions, particularly those of the Mende, Yoruba, Kongo, and Akan peoples. In West and Central Africa, wood carving served religious, social, and utilitarian purposes. Masks, ancestor figures, and ritual objects were central to spiritual practices, while tools like rice paddles and stools reflected daily life.  

### **Survival and Transformation in the Americas**  
Enslaved Africans brought these skills to the Lowcountry, where they adapted to new materials and needs. Isolated on plantations and sea islands, the Gullah Geechee maintained African carving techniques while incorporating European influences. For example, sweetgrass basketry and wood carving became intertwined, with craftsmen using local woods like cypress, oak, and cedar.  

**Key Figures:**  
- **Philip Simmons (1912–2009)**: A Charleston blacksmith and woodcarver whose work incorporated African motifs.  
- **Sam Doyle (1906–1985)**: A St. Helena Island artist who blended wood carving with painted folk art.  

## **2. Tools, Materials, and Techniques**  

### **Traditional Tools**  
Early Gullah Geechee carvers used simple, hand-forged tools:  
- **Adzes and drawknives** for shaping logs.  
- **Chisels and mallets** for fine detailing.  
- **Whittling knives** for smaller pieces.  

### **Local Woods and Their Significance**  
- **Cypress**: Resistant to rot, used for outdoor items like mortars and boat figureheads.  
- **Live Oak**: Durable, employed for tools and furniture.  
- **Sweetgum and Cedar**: Chosen for spiritual carvings due to their aromatic properties.  

### **Carving Techniques**  
- **Relief Carving**: Common in decorative panels and storytelling pieces.  
- **In-the-Round Sculpture**: Used for figurines and ceremonial objects.  
- **Chip Carving**: Geometric patterns reminiscent of African textiles.  

## **3. Symbolism and Spiritual Significance**  

### **Ancestral Veneration and Protection**  
Many carvings serve as conduits for spiritual communication. Examples include:  
- **"Conjure Sticks"**: Carved staffs used in rootwork and healing traditions.  
- **"Face Jugs"**: Pottery and wood carvings with exaggerated features to ward off evil spirits.  

### **Narrative and Folklore**  
Carvers often depict scenes from Gullah Geechee folktales, such as:  
- **Bruh Rabbit and Bruh Fox**: Trickster tales preserved in three-dimensional form.  
- **Haint Blue Doors**: Carved doors painted blue to repel malevolent spirits (a practice tied to Yoruba indigo symbolism).  

## **4. Functional Craftsmanship: From Daily Use to Artistic Mastery**  

### **Household and Agricultural Implements**  
- **Rice Pestles and Mortars**: Essential for processing Carolina Gold rice.  
- **Fishing Decoys**: Used in tidal creek fishing traditions.  

### **Boat Carving and Maritime Traditions**  
Gullah Geechee boatbuilders carved figureheads and net gauges, reflecting their expertise in seafaring—a skill tracing back to West African canoe culture.  

## **5. The Decline and Revival of Gullah Geechee Wood Carving**  

### **20th Century Challenges**  
Industrialization and land dispossession threatened traditional crafts. Many carvers turned to tourism-related work, producing "souvenir art" rather than culturally significant pieces.  

### **Contemporary Revival**  
Organizations like the **Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor** and **Penn Center** have supported carvers through apprenticeships and exhibitions. Notable modern carvers include:  
- **James Garfield Smalls**: A St. Helena Island carver specializing in figurative sculpture.  
- **Ronald Daise**: A cultural preservationist who documents carving traditions.  

## **Conclusion**  
Gullah Geechee wood carving is a living tradition that bridges Africa and the Americas. From spiritual artifacts to functional tools, these carvings embody resilience, creativity, and cultural continuity. As contemporary artists reclaim and reinterpret these practices, the legacy of Gullah Geechee craftsmanship endures as a testament to survival and identity.
