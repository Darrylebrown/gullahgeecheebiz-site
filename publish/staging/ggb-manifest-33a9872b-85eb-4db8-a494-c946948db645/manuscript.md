# **Sea Island Architecture and Building Traditions**  
**Volume 23 of the Gullah Geechee Encyclopedia Series**  

## **Introduction**  
The architecture of the Sea Islands, stretching from North Carolina to Florida, offers a profound testament to the resilience, ingenuity, and cultural preservation of the Gullah Geechee people. Descendants of West and Central Africans brought to the coastal Southeast as enslaved laborers, the Gullah Geechee developed unique building traditions that merged African techniques with locally available materials. From tabby-walled homes to elevated "shotgun" houses, their structures embodied both practical adaptations to the subtropical environment and deep-rooted cultural memory. These traditions persist today in historic communities such as Sapelo Island (Georgia), Daufuskie Island (South Carolina), and St. Helena Island (South Carolina), where elders still recount the oral histories of these structures.  

This chapter explores the key features, materials, and sociocultural significance of Sea Island architecture, documenting how enslaved and later freed communities created sustainable living spaces under oppressive conditions. Whether through the use of oyster shell tabby, palmetto thatching, or spiritually protective design elements, Gullah Geechee building practices reflect an unbroken connection to ancestral knowledge.

## **1. Historical Foundations: African Retentions in Sea Island Architecture**  
The architectural traditions of the Sea Islands were heavily influenced by West and Central African building techniques, particularly those from Sierra Leone, Ghana, and Angola. The "wattle-and-daub" method—interlacing wooden frames with an earthen plaster—was common in Africa and adapted in the Lowcountry using marsh clay and oyster shells. Enslaved Africans also brought knowledge of elevated structures (to prevent flooding) and open-air ventilation systems, critical in the humid Sea Island climate.  

Archaeological evidence from the 18th and 19th centuries, such as remnants of cabins on Ossabaw Island (Georgia), shows circular or rectangular layouts consistent with African compound designs. These structures were often clustered in "slave streets," where extended families occupied interconnected dwellings, reinforcing communal living practices.  

## **2. Tabby Construction: From Enslaved Labor to Community Identity**  
One of the most enduring architectural legacies is **tabby**—a concrete-like material made from burned oyster shells, sand, water, and lime. Introduced by Spanish colonizers but perfected by enslaved laborers, tabby was used in foundations, walls, and chimneys. Plantation owners forced enslaved people to gather millions of oyster shells from **middens** (indigenous shell mounds) for large-scale projects, such as the Wormsloe Plantation near Savannah.  

After emancipation, Gullah Geechee communities repurposed tabby for their own homes and churches. The **First African Baptist Church (1859) on St. Simons Island**, one of the oldest Black congregations in the U.S., features tabby walls, demonstrating its lasting cultural significance.  

## **3. The Shotgun House: Form, Function, and Spiritual Protection**  
A defining feature of Sea Island settlements is the **shotgun house**—a narrow, rectangular dwelling with rooms aligned in a row, allowing cross-ventilation. Though often associated with Creole culture, the shotgun’s origins trace back to Yoruba and Dahomeyan designs brought via the transatlantic slave trade.  

Gullah Geechee tradition holds that these homes should face eastward to honor ancestral spirits. Additionally, **blue paint on porch ceilings**—a custom still seen in Charleston and Beaufort—was believed to ward off "haints" (malevolent spirits), a practice linked to Central African spiritual systems.  

## **4. Thatched Roofs and Natural Materials**  
Before tin roofs became common, Gullah Geechee builders used **palmetto fronds** and **sea grass** for thatching. This practice, derived from West African techniques, provided insulation and rainwater runoff. Later, **sweetgrass baskets**, coiled like those in Sierra Leone, were repurposed as roof insulation in some homes.  

"Praise houses" (small chapels used for nighttime worship) often featured steeply pitched roofs to channel heavy rains—a design still replicated in modern Gullah-built chapels.  

## **5. The Role of the Black Carpenter: Craftsmanship and Resistance**  
Enslaved and freed Black carpenters were pivotal to Sea Island architecture. Skilled artisans like **March Gardner**, who helped build the tabby ruins on St. Helena Island, demonstrated how craftsmanship served as a form of economic and cultural resistance. After the Civil War, Black carpenters constructed entire freedmen’s villages, including **Mitchelville (Hilton Head, 1862)**, the first self-governed Black town in the South.  

Oral histories from Sapelo Island elders describe **"basket houses"**—small dwellings with woven walls—showing the adaptability of Gullah builders under resource constraints.  

## **6. Preservation and Modern Challenges**  
Since the 20th century, Gullah Geechee architectural heritage has faced threats from **tourism-driven development, rising sea levels, and gentrification**. Activists like **Emory Campbell (St. Helena Island)** have fought to protect historic homes from demolition, while organizations like the **Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor Commission** document at-risk structures.  

Efforts to revive traditional methods include **tabby-making workshops** in Georgia and **sweetgrass-roofing demonstrations** at the Penn Center. However, material scarcity (e.g., oyster shell depletion) poses challenges for restoration.  

---  

## **Conclusion**  
Sea Island architecture stands as a living archive of Gullah Geechee resilience, blending ancestral knowledge with environmental necessity. Each tabby wall, thatched roof, and shotgun doorway carries centuries of history, from the forced labor of plantation building to the self-determined craftsmanship of freedmen’s villages. Today, preserving these structures is not just about heritage conservation but also about honoring the ingenuity of a people who built communities against all odds.
