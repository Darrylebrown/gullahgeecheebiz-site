# **The Civil War in the Sea Islands**  

## **Introduction**  

The Sea Islands of South Carolina and Georgia became a crucial theater of the American Civil War (1861–1865), not only for military strategy but also as a site of liberation and resistance for the Gullah Geechee people—enslaved Africans and their descendants who had shaped the region’s culture and labor force for centuries. When Union forces captured the Sea Islands in late 1861, thousands of Gullah Geechee people seized their freedom, transforming the war into a struggle for their own emancipation and autonomy.  

This chapter examines the Civil War's impact on the Sea Islands through a Gullah Geechee lens, exploring military campaigns, the Port Royal Experiment, black military service, and the cultural resilience of the people. These events laid the foundation for Reconstruction and the long fight for land rights and self-determination.  

## **1. The Fall of the Sea Islands: The Union Invasion of 1861**  

On November 7, 1861, Union naval forces, led by Admiral Samuel F. Du Pont, launched a successful attack on Port Royal Sound, seizing Beaufort, Hilton Head, and St. Helena Island. The swift Confederate retreat left behind plantations and over 10,000 enslaved Gullah Geechee people, who immediately claimed their freedom.  

White planters fled inland, abandoning homes, crops, and personal belongings. The formerly enslaved, many of whom had been raising cotton, rice, and indigo, refused to follow their enslavers, instead welcoming Union soldiers as liberators. Charlotte Forten, a Black abolitionist who later taught on St. Helena Island, wrote in her journal: *“The people welcomed the Yankees with tears of joy. They had escaped bondage—now they would fight to keep their freedom.”*  

This moment marked one of the first large-scale emancipations of the Civil War, setting the stage for the Union’s evolving policies on slavery.  

## **2. The Port Royal Experiment: A Laboratory for Freedom**  

In the wake of Union occupation, the federal government initiated the "Port Royal Experiment," a radical social and economic program to transition the Sea Islands from slavery to free labor. Northern missionaries, teachers, and officials—many affiliated with abolitionist organizations—came to assist the freedmen.  

Key components of the experiment included:  
- **Land redistribution:** Abandoned plantations were leased to freedmen, allowing them to farm independently.  
- **Education:** Schools were established, including the Penn School on St. Helena (founded in 1862), one of the first schools for freedmen in the South.  
- **Black self-governance:** Freedmen organized their own communities, with some serving as local magistrates.  

Gullah Geechee people took full advantage of these opportunities, demonstrating their readiness for citizenship. However, tensions arose when some Northern reformers imposed paternalistic attitudes, while others, like abolitionist Laura Towne, worked in genuine partnership with the people.  

## **3. Gullah Geechee Soldiers: The Founding of the First South Carolina Volunteers**  

The Union army quickly recognized the strategic importance of recruiting Black soldiers in the Sea Islands. In May 1862, the **1st South Carolina Volunteer Infantry (Colored)**, one of the first official Black Union regiments, was formed under Colonel Thomas Wentworth Higginson, a white abolitionist. The regiment comprised mostly Gullah Geechee men, including **Robert Smalls**, who had famously commandeered the Confederate ship *Planter* in 1862 and delivered it to Union forces.  

The soldiers played a crucial role in:  
- **Guerrilla warfare:** Conducting raids along the Georgia and South Carolina coast.  
- **Liberating the enslaved:** Encouraging mass escapes from plantations.  
- **Proving Black valor:** Defying stereotypes through battlefield courage, as seen at the Battle of Honey Hill (1864).  

Their service strengthened arguments for emancipation and Black citizenship, directly influencing Lincoln’s issuance of the Emancipation Proclamation (1863).  

## **4. Confederate Retaliation and Gullah Geechee Resistance**  

The Confederate military attempted to reclaim the Sea Islands, resulting in violent skirmishes. Guerilla bands, such as those led by Confederate Colonel John J. Pettigrew, burned freedmen’s homes and destroyed crops.  

However, Gullah Geechee communities fought back by:  
- **Providing intelligence to Union forces.**  
- **Sheltering escapees from inland plantations.**  
- **Maintaining secret communication networks among enslaved laborers still under Confederate control.**  

Oral histories from St. Helena Island recount how elders advised young men to join the Union army, while women hid supplies and cared for the wounded.  

## **5. Land and Legacy: The Promise and Betrayal of “40 Acres and a Mule”**  

In January 1865, General William Tecumseh Sherman issued **Special Field Orders No. 15**, which reserved land along the coast—from Charleston to the St. John’s River in Florida—for freedmen. This was the origin of the phrase “40 acres and a mule.” Thousands of Gullah Geechee families received land titles, many of which were later revoked after President Andrew Johnson’s 1865 pardons allowed former Confederates to reclaim property.  

Despite this betrayal, some families, particularly on St. Helena and Sapelo Islands, retained land through persistent legal battles and communal cooperation. These landholdings became the foundation of enduring Gullah Geechee cultural preservation.  

## **6. Cultural Resilience: Gullah Geechee Life During and After the War**  

Throughout the war, Gullah Geechee people sustained their traditions:  
- **African spiritual practices** were maintained secretly, blending with Christianity.  
- **Foodways**, such as rice cultivation and seafood harvesting, ensured survival.  
- **Oral storytelling** documented wartime heroism, ensuring collective memory.  

Freedmen’s churches, like **Brick Baptist Church on St. Helena (1855)**, became community centers for organizing and education.  

## **Conclusion**  

The Civil War in the Sea Islands was not merely a military campaign but a revolutionary struggle for freedom led by the Gullah Geechee people. Their resistance, military service, and determination to claim land and education set a precedent for Reconstruction and the broader Civil Rights Movement. Though the promise of true equality remained unfulfilled, the war marked the beginning of a long fight for autonomy, one that continues today in efforts to preserve Gullah Geechee land and culture.
