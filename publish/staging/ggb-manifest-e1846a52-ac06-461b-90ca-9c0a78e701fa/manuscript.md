# **Volume 35: Gullah Geechee Proverbs and Wisdom Traditions**  

## **Introduction**  
The Gullah Geechee people, descendants of enslaved Africans who settled in the coastal regions of South Carolina, Georgia, Florida, and North Carolina, have preserved a rich oral tradition that encapsulates their history, values, and worldview. Proverbs, folktales, and wisdom sayings serve as cultural cornerstones, transmitting knowledge across generations while resisting the erasure of African heritage. Rooted in West and Central African linguistic and philosophical traditions, Gullah Geechee proverbs emphasize communal responsibility, resilience, and spiritual insight. This chapter examines the origins, themes, and enduring significance of these wisdom traditions, highlighting their role in sustaining Gullah Geechee identity amid displacement and cultural suppression.  

## **1. African Roots and Linguistic Continuity**  
Gullah Geechee proverbs retain strong linguistic and philosophical ties to West African languages such as Mende, Wolof, Kikongo, and Yoruba. Enslaved Africans, forcibly transported to the Sea Islands and Lowcountry regions between the 17th and 19th centuries, blended these linguistic elements with English, creating a distinct Creole language. Proverbs like *"Trouble don’ las’ always"* (Mende: *"Pɔli gbaŋ gbɛɛ"*) and *"Ef you wan’ know how ‘e go, aks dem wha’ been dey"* (Yoruba: *"Ẹni tó bá fẹ́ mọ ọ̀nà, kí ó béèrè ọ̀nà lọ́nà"*) illustrate this continuity.  

Scholars such as Lorenzo Dow Turner, author of *Africanisms in the Gullah Dialect* (1949), identified direct parallels between African proverbs and Gullah Geechee sayings, demonstrating how enslaved people preserved ancestral wisdom despite linguistic oppression.  

## **2. Themes of Resilience and Survival**  
Many Gullah Geechee proverbs reflect a philosophy of endurance and adaptability. Sayings like *"De higher de monkey climb, de more ‘e show ‘e tail"* (a warning against arrogance) and *"Hard time make monkey eat pepper"* (resilience in adversity) reveal a worldview shaped by slavery and Jim Crow.  

During the Reconstruction era, figures like Robert Smalls—a formerly enslaved Gullah man who became a U.S. Congressman—invoked proverbs to inspire collective resistance. Oral histories collected by the Works Progress Administration (WPA) in the 1930s further document how proverbs reinforced solidarity among Black communities facing systemic violence.  

## **3. Nature and Ancestral Wisdom**  
The Gullah Geechee’s deep connection to the land and sea is reflected in proverbs that draw on natural imagery. *"Tide don’ wait on no man"* underscores the inevitability of change, while *"Crab walk sideways, but ‘e reach de same place"* speaks to indirect but effective strategies for survival.  

These sayings mirror African cosmological traditions, where animals and natural phenomena symbolize moral lessons. The influence of Akan and Igbo storytelling is evident in tales like *Brer Rabbit*, which, like Anansi stories, use animal allegories to critique power structures.  

## **4. Community and Ethical Teachings**  
Proverbs emphasizing communal ethics—such as *"One han’ wash de odder"* (mutual aid) and *"Wha’ sweeten goat mouth, ‘e gwine burn ‘e tail"* (consequences of selfishness)—reinforce collective well-being over individualism. This reflects West African communal values, where proverbs serve as social regulators.  

Gullah Geechee elders, known as *"root doctors"* or *"cultural griots,"* historically used proverbs to guide youth. For example, during the Penn School’s founding (1862), one of the first schools for freedmen, teachers incorporated proverbs into lessons to instill moral discipline.  

## **5. Spirituality and the Supernatural**  
Many proverbs intersect with Gullah Geechee spiritual beliefs, blending Christianity with African cosmology. *"God don’ like ugly"* condemns moral corruption, while *"De dead ain’t dead"* reflects ancestral veneration, akin to the Yoruba concept of *egúngún* (ancestors).  

Hoodoo and rootwork traditions also employ proverbs as protective incantations. For instance, *"Ef you dig a ditch fo’ somebody, dig two"* warns against malice, echoing the Kongo principle of *"Simbi"* (spiritual balance).  

## **6. Modern Revitalization and Cultural Preservation**  
Despite mid-20th century displacement due to land grabs and tourism development, Gullah Geechee proverbs persist through cultural revitalization efforts. Organizations like the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor Commission (established 2006) and storytellers like Aunt Pearlie Sue (Dr. Patricia Jones-Jackson) have amplified these traditions.  

Contemporary artists, such as singer-songwriter Ranky Tanky, incorporate proverbs into music, ensuring their relevance. Academic projects, including the *Gullah Language and Folklore Project* (University of South Carolina), continue documenting these sayings as living artifacts.  

## **Conclusion**  
Gullah Geechee proverbs are more than linguistic relics—they are dynamic instruments of cultural survival. By weaving African heritage with adaptive wisdom, they offer timeless guidance on justice, community, and resilience. As the Gullah Geechee people navigate modern challenges, these oral traditions remain vital to preserving their identity and sovereignty.
