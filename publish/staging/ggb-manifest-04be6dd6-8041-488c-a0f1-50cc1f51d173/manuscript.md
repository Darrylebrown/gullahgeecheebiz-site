## Chapter 7.1: Bunce Island: Architecture & Operation

Bunce Island, located in the Sierra Leone River estuary approximately 20 miles upstream from Freetown, operated as a British slave castle from approximately 1670 to 1807. The fortification consists of "two semi-circular bastions connected by a long curtain wall" originally built to hold sixteen guns, later reduced to eight.

### The Factory House
The "Bance Island House"—the two-story factory house—was built in the tropical Georgian style with an elevated veranda wrapping around three sides, architecturally mirroring the homes of South Carolina Lowcountry rice planters. British naturalist Henry Smeathman described it in 1773 as "a plain, neat building enough with a very cool and convenient gallery."

### The Supply Network
African captives arrived at Bunce Island through multiple channels:
1. Sloop captains from out-factories up to 300 miles away
2. Mandingo, Susu, and Fula middlemen from the interior
3. Euro-African traders from the coastal region

### Archaeological Evidence
Joseph Opala's 1976 archaeological survey identified "concentrations of artifacts, particularly on the slope beneath the fortification where the slave traders consistently tossed their rubbish," including European ceramics, African pottery, gunflints, and glass beads.

## Chapter 7.2: The Bunce Island-Charleston Connection

Bunce Island was "the principal supplier of slaves to the Charlestown [South Carolina] market throughout the mid- and late 18th century." The connection was not merely commercial but ecological: both regions specialized in rice cultivation, and Carolina planters specifically sought captives from the Rice Coast for their agricultural expertise.

### Documented Ship Routes
- The *Hare* (1756) — Priscilla's journey
- The *Lucky Galley* (1756) — permit from Governor James Glen
- Multiple South Sea Company vessels (1713-1750)
- New England-based schooners (1750s-1760s)

## Chapter 7.3: UNESCO World Heritage Status

Bunce Island holds Outstanding Universal Value under Criterion (ii) as "tangibly associated with the expansion and deepening of rice cultivation in America." The nomination document states: "The castle's involvement in the North American trade is of special interest to people in the United States, since the ancestors of many African Americans came from this part of West Africa."

## Chapter 7.4: The Homecomings

### 1989: Emory Campbell's Delegation
Fourteen Gullah delegates visited Sierra Leone for a week of national celebration. President Momoh spoke to them in Krio, and the similarity between the two languages was immediately apparent. Emory Campbell was "anointed on his forehead with the soil of Bunce Island."

### 1997: The Moran Family
Mary Moran visited the Mende village where her family's funeral song is still sung. Her son Wilson stood in the slave yard and said: "I feel a lot of pain here, a lot of pain... what man can do to man."

### 2005: Priscilla's Return
Thomalind Polite, 7th-generation descendant of Priscilla (age 10 on the *Hare*, 1756), arrived in Sierra Leone. "When Mrs. Polite entered the slave yard for women and children, she said she could feel Priscilla's presence by 'walking where she walked.'"
