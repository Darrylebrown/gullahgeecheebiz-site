## COVER STORY
### The Block That Shook the World

**By Marcus Chen, Senior Volleyball Correspondent**

When Italian powerhouse **Novara** faced Turkish giants **VakifBank Istanbul** on Saturday, August 16th, at the PalaVerde in Vicenza, nobody expected a match that would be talked about for decades. What happened over five brutal sets — a combined 118 points, 47 aces, and a block celebration that looked more like a playoff than a regular-season exhibition — changed how the volleyball world sees the emerging generation.

The decisive moment came in the fifth set at 14-14. Novara's 22-year-old middle blocker, **Giulia Moretti**, read the play before it developed. Turkish setter **Elif Kaya** went for her go-to quick — a snap set to outside hitter **Brenda Costa** — but Moretti was already airborne. Two hands, one perfect timing, and the ball kissed the floor on the VakifBank side. The arena erupted. Novara won 16-14, completing one of the most incredible comeback victories in women's club volleyball history.

"We practiced that read for six weeks," Moretti said afterward, still catching her breath. "Coach told us Brenda always checks the left corner before her approach. We knew she was going to be there."

The stats tell the story: Moretti recorded 9 blocks — the single-season record for a match in the Club World Championship qualifiers. Her teammate **Sofia Bergman** added 7 more kills in the fifth set alone. For VakifBank, it was a heartbreaking loss after leading 2-0 in sets. Their head coach **Marc Llorens** praised Novara's adaptability: "They came out of the timeout different. That's championship-level composure."

The implications are enormous. Novara, currently sitting third in the FIVB World Ranking, has now won three consecutive matches against top-5 opponents. Their path to the **2026 Club World Championship in Tokyo** (September 12–18) just got a whole lot clearer. For VakifBank, this is a wake-up call — and a reminder that in modern volleyball, no lead is safe until the ball hits the floor.

## PLAYER SPOTLIGHT
### The Rise of Kenji Tanaka: Japan's New Outside Hitter Prince

**By Yuki Watanabe**

If you missed **Kenji Tanaka** (age 21, 6'4") at the Asian Continental Cup earlier this month, you missed something special. Playing for the **Panasonic Panthers** in the V.League, Tanaka has become the most exciting young outside hitter in Asian volleyball — and he's just getting started.

Tanaka's game is built on three pillars: a 385cm vertical leap, pinpoint serve-receive accuracy, and a powerful jump float that has defenders diving for balls that shouldn't be difficult. His numbers from the 2025–2026 V.League season are staggering: 14.7 kills per set, a .312 hitting percentage, and 98 aces — the second-highest total in league history for a rookie.

"What makes Kenji different is his intelligence," said Panthers head coach **Hiroshi Nakamura**. "He doesn't just hit harder. He sees the defense before it's set. That's something you can't coach."

Tanaka's journey began in Okinawa, where he played beach volleyball until age 15. When he transitioned to indoor, coaches were skeptical — his technique needed work. But his work ethic won out. He arrived at the National Training Center in Tokyo with a rep for being the first to practice and the last to leave.

Don't sleep on him. By 2028, when the **Paris Olympics** rolls around (July 25 – August 10), Tanaka could be carrying Japan's hopes on his shoulders.

## TIPS & TRAINING
### The 3-Step Serve Return Revolution

**By Maria Santos, High-Performance Coach**

Every coach knows the serve receive is where matches are won and lost. But most players practice it wrong. Here's the three-step system we use with our national team that has reduced reception errors by 34% this season.

**Step 1: Read the Server's Approach**
Watch the server's last two steps. Are they opening up early? That's a cross-court serve coming. Are they staying closed? Expect line. Give yourself 0.3 seconds of processing time before the ball is even struck.

**Step 2: Platform First, Eyes Second**
Most young players look at the ball before setting their platform. Wrong. Set your arms first, then track the ball. This simple change alone will eliminate 80% of your shanks. Your platform should be solid before the ball crosses the net — not after.

**Step 3: Call It or Commit**
If you call "mine" late, you've already lost. Call early, call loud, and commit. Your teammate needs to know whether to rotate or stay. Hesitation is the enemy.

**Practice drill:** Have your setter stand behind you and call serves randomly. You must call "mine" or "yours" before the server strikes. If you hesitate, you do 10 burpees. It's uncomfortable — and that's the point.

## HISTORY LESSON
### The Day the Net Changed Forever

**By Robert Hayes, Volleyball Historian**

On **August 20, 1964**, during the Tokyo Olympics, the world watched something unprecedented: the first-ever women's volleyball final broadcast live in color. But the match itself — **Cuba vs. Japan** — would become legendary for a different reason.

Cuba, led by the towering **Marisela Martínez** (6'1" for her era), had never lost a set in the tournament. Japan, coached by the legendary **Shigeo Mori**, was known for their speed and precision. What happened in five sets changed how the sport was played forever.

The turning point came in set four at 14-14. Japan's **Fumiko Okuno** executed what commentators would later call "the impossible dig" — a one-handed pancake save off a Martinez spike that looked like it had no chance. The ball barely cleared the net, and Japan scored the point.

"That dig taught us that volleyball isn't just about height," Mori said decades later. "It's about heart. You can train technique. You can't train the will to stay on the floor when everyone else has given up."

Japan won that set 16-14 and went on to claim the gold medal — ending Cuba's 32-match Olympic winning streak. It was the first time an Asian women's team had won Olympic volleyball gold, and it inspired a generation of players across the continent.

Today, 62 years later, when a 5'8" setter blocks a 6'5" hitter at the net, or a libero makes a one-handed save that defies physics, we're still living in the shadow of that August day in Tokyo. The sport has evolved — the libero arrived in 1998, the rally point system in 1999, and the 24-second shot clock in 2023 — but the fundamental truth remains: volleyball is not won by the tallest. It's won by the brave.
