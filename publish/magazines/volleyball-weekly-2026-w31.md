## COVER STORY
### The Polish Powerhouse: Projekt Warszawa Clinches CEV Champions League in Stunning Fashion

**By Marcus Delgado, Senior Volleyball Correspondent**

The Palais Omnisports de Paris-Bercy will be buzzing long after the final whistle, but none more so than the players of Projekt Warszawa, who captured the 2026 CEV Men's Champions League title on Saturday night in a breathtaking five-set thriller against Italy's Sir Safety Conad Perugia.

The final scoreline—25–22, 21–25, 25–20, 20–25, 15–13—tells only part of the story. What made this match unforgettable was the sheer resilience displayed by a Polish side that had never before lifted the CEV Champions League trophy, despite finishing as runners-up on three previous occasions.

"It was a career-defining moment for every single one of us," said veteran setter Kamil Semeniuk, named MVP of the final. "We've been chasing this since we were kids playing in community halls back in Gdańsk. To do it on the biggest stage, against the best team in the world, means everything."

Semeniuk's performance was nothing short of surgical. He logged 48 assists in a match where Perugia's formidable block—led by world-class blocker Fabio Balaso—limited him to no easy sets. His dump shots in critical moments kept Perugia's blockers honest, and his reading of the Italian attack in the fifth set proved to be the difference.

The turning point came in the fourth set at 17–16, when Projekt's outside hitter Aleksander Żuk unleashed a cross-court kill that left Perugia's libero Michele Sartori rooted to the floor. That single point shifted the momentum decisively. Żuk finished the match with 22 points, 14 of them on attacks, and his leadership in the huddles between sets was noted by analysts as a factor in Projekt's composure during the decider.

Perugia, making their fourth Champions League final in six years, had expected to complete a historic back-to-to-back title run. Head coach Andrea Giani, a Italian volleyball legend in his own right, praised his team's effort but acknowledged Projekt's superior adaptation.

"We came out too cautious in the third and fifth sets," Giani admitted. "Projekt pressed us early and often, and we couldn't find our rhythm. That's the difference at this level—marginally better decisions at crucial moments."

The victory sends Projekt Warszawa through to next season's Club World Championship, where they will represent Europe alongside reigning FIVB Champions League winners. For the club, founded in 2013 by businessman Dariusz Daszczyk as a passion project to bring elite volleyball to northern Poland, this triumph represents the culmination of over a decade of investment in youth development, facilities, and sporting direction.

"The identity of this club has always been about believing you can compete with anyone, anywhere," said general manager Marcin Mierzejewski. "Tonight, the whole of Poland celebrated. That's the real prize."

## PLAYER SPOTLIGHT
### Maya Tran: The Rising Star Defining a New Generation

**Profiled by Chloe Tanaka, Features Editor**

At just 21 years old, Maya Tran has already become the face of women's professional volleyball in North America. The 6'2" outside hitter, currently starring for the Vancouver Vixens in the revamped North American Volleyball League (NAVL), is on pace to break the single-season kill record set in 2019.

Tran's ascent has been meteoric. Drafted third overall in the 2023 NAVL Draft out of the University of Southern California, she has averaged 14.7 points per set this season—well above the league average of 9.3—and leads all NAVL players with a .387 hitting percentage. But numbers alone don't capture what makes Tran special.

"It's her court sense," said Vancouver head coach Diana Rosales. "Most players her age are still figuring out where everyone is on the floor. Maya knows where everyone will be three seconds from now. That's rare."

Born in Vancouver to Vietnamese-Canadian parents, Tran began playing volleyball at age seven at a community center in Richmond, B.C. She was overlooked by many scouts for much of her youth career—partly because she was undersized for her age group, partly because she played in a market that wasn't a traditional volleyball powerhouse. Her breakthrough came at the 2022 FIVB U20 World Championship, where she led Team Canada to its first-ever medal (bronze) and was named Best Outside Hitter.

Since then, her game has evolved into a complete offensive weapon. Her jump serve now clocks in at 92 km/h consistently, and her roll serve—once a liability—has been transformed into a genuine scoring shot under the guidance of Vancouver's passing coach, former Romanian international Anca Popescu.

Off the court, Tran has become a vocal advocate for diversity in volleyball. She recently launched the "Set for Change" foundation, which provides volleyball equipment and coaching to underserved youth programs across British Columbia. In its first year, the foundation has reached over 1,200 children.

"People ask me all the time what my goal is," Tran said in a recent interview. "My goal is simple: I want the next kid who looks like me—who doesn't come from a traditional volleyball family—to pick up a ball and know that this sport is for them too."

With the 2028 Los Angeles Olympics on the horizon, Tran is already being discussed as a cornerstone of the Canadian senior national team. If she continues on this trajectory, the conversation could shift from "if" to "when"—and when that day comes, the world will be watching.

## GAME ANALYSIS
### Breaking Down the Derby: Brno Defeats Prague 3–2 in Czech League Classic

**By Tomáš Hrdlička, Tactical Analyst**

The Czech Volleyball Extraliga fixture between VK Kladno Praha and ČEZ Karlovarsko Brno on Thursday night will be remembered as one of the most compelling matches of the domestic season. Brno's 25–20, 22–25, 20–25, 25–18, 15–11 victory showcased tactical flexibility, defensive grit, and some of the highest-quality volleyball seen in Central Europe this year.

**First Set: Brno's Block Reads the Offensive Playbook**

Brno came out with a deliberate strategy to neutralize Prague's primary weapon: outside hitter Dominik Černý. Captain and head coach Michal Novák deployed a double-block on Černý from the outset, forcing Prague to either go through him or adapt. The Prague coaching staff, led by veteran Jiří Svoboda, struggled to adjust. Brno's middle blockers, led by 6'7" David Vondráček, timed their jumps perfectly against Prague's quick sets, resulting in four blocks and seven more touches at the net in the opening frame.

**Second and Third Sets: Prague Finds Its Rhythm**

Prague's adjustments were sharp. Svoboda shifted to a more balanced attack, distributing the ball to opposite hitter Tomáš Neuwirth, who exploited the seams in Brno's defense for eight points across the two sets. Brno's defensive formation—typically a 5-1 zone—was pulled apart as Prague hit .412 on the night's middle sets.

The critical moment came at 22–21 in the third set, when Prague's libero Petra Kučerová dug a powerful cross-court spike from Brno's Jan Drozd, keeping the rally alive. Prague ultimately closed the set 25–20, taking a 1–2 series deficit heading into the fourth.

**Fourth and Fifth Sets: Brno's Mental Fortitude Prevails**

What separated Brno was its composure under pressure. Down 9–7 in the fourth set, Novák called a timeout and made a substitution that changed the match: bringing on defensive specialist Lenka Šimáčková for a front-row attack, a move that caught Prague off guard. Šimáčková's serve ace and subsequent kill shifted the set's trajectory, and Brno won 25–18.

The fifth set was a masterclass in error minimization. Both teams combined for only six unforced errors in 22 minutes of play. Brno's leadership shone through, with Novák's timeout at 12–11—calling a quick middle attack that resulted in a kill—demonstrating the calm decision-making that has defined his coaching career.

**Key Statistics:**
- Brno: 18 blocks, 12 aces, .298 hitting percentage
- Prague: 14 blocks, 9 aces, .312 hitting percentage
- Match duration: 2 hours, 47 minutes
- Jan Drozd (Brno): 28 points, .401 hitting percentage
- Dominik Černý (Prague): 24 points, .344 hitting percentage

This victory keeps Brno in first place in the Extraliga standings, two points ahead of Prague with three matches remaining in the regular season.

## TIPS & TRAINING
### Mastering the Jump Serve: A Comprehensive Guide

**By Coach Rachel Okonkwo, National Team Staff**

The jump serve is the most effective serving technique in modern volleyball. It combines power, disguise, and precision in a way that flat and float serves simply cannot match. Whether you're a beginner looking to add a new weapon to your arsenal or an advanced player refining your technique, this guide will walk you through the essentials.

**1. The Toss: Your Foundation**

Everything begins with the toss. A consistent toss is the single most important factor in jump-serve success. Practice tossing the ball to the same height and location every time. The ideal toss for a jump serve is slightly in front of your hitting shoulder and about 12–18 inches above your full extension. Too far back, and you'll lose power; too far forward, and you'll reach and compromise your contact.

*Drill:* Stand at the service line and practice your toss without hitting. Toss and catch at the peak of your jump. Repeat until the toss is identical on every attempt.

**2. The Approach: Three-Step Rhythm**

The most common approach is the three-step: right-left-right for right-handed servers (reverse for lefties). The first step is a small, preparatory step with your non-hitting-side foot. The second step is wider and longer, generating momentum. The third step is your plant and jump—your feet should come together under your body, and your arms should swing forward and up to generate lift.

*Key point:* Your last step should be explosive. Think of it as a "check" step that stops your forward momentum and converts it into vertical lift.

**3. The Contact: Whip It Through**

At the peak of your jump, your hitting arm should be drawn back with your elbow at shoulder height and your wrist cocked. Your non-hitting arm should point at the ball to aid balance and direction. Contact should be made with the heel of your palm or the base of your fingers, wrapping around the ball to create spin.

*Common mistake:* Hitting the ball with the tip of your fingers or a stiff wrist. This produces a weak, predictable serve. Think of your hand as a whip—loose until the moment of contact, then tight.

**4. The Disguise: Keep It Honest**

Elite jump servers disguise their target until the last possible moment. Your body language, approach, and toss should look identical regardless of where you intend to serve. Only at the point of contact should your aim become clear.

**5. Progression for Beginners**

If you're new to the jump serve:
- Start with a float serve to develop your hitting technique
- Practice your toss and approach without jumping
- Add a two-step approach from a standing start
- Progress to a full three-step jump serve
- Focus on consistency over power—get 5 out of 10 serves in before adding pace

Remember: mastery takes time. Even professionals spend hours each week refining their jump serve. Be patient, be deliberate, and trust the process.

## HISTORY LESSON
### August 9, 1964: Volleyball Makes Its Olympic Debut in Tokyo

**By Professor Elena Vasquez, Sports Historian**

The year was 1964, and the world was gathering in Tokyo for the Games of the XVIII Olympiad. Among the 110 events on the program was a sport that had been invented just 75 years earlier by William G. Morgan in Holyoke, Massachusetts. Volleyball's Olympic debut would go down as a milestone not just for the sport, but for the global recognition of women's athletics.

**The Men's Tournament**

The men's competition featured twelve teams, including the Soviet Union, Japan, Czechoslovakia, Poland, and the United States. The Soviet team, led by coach Nikolay Karpol, was the overwhelming favorite. Japan, the host nation, had something to prove on home soil.

The final between the USSR and Japan remains one of the most dramatic matches in Olympic history. The Soviets, who had won the world championship in 1962, took the first two sets convincingly. But Japan, playing with the roar of a home crowd behind them, fought back to win the third and fourth sets. The decider was a grueling affair, with the Soviets eventually prevailing 15–10 to claim gold. Japan took silver, and Poland completed the podium.

**A Historic Moment for Women's Volleyball**

Perhaps more significant than the men's tournament was the women's competition—the first time women's volleyball appeared at the Olympics. Eight nations competed, and the Soviet Union, once again, was dominant. They won all five of their matches without dropping a set, defeating Japan 3–0 in the final.

For many nations, this was their first opportunity to showcase women's volleyball on the world stage. The visibility provided by the Olympic platform helped accelerate the sport's growth in countries that had previously limited women's participation in team sports.

**The Legacy**

Seventeen years after its Olympic debut, volleyball would become the only team sport in which the Soviet Union won every Olympic gold medal from 1964 to 1988 (the USSR boycotted the 1984 Games). The sport has since grown into one of the world's most popular, with an estimated 800 million people playing recreationally worldwide.

On this day in 1964, a game born in a YMCA gymnasium in Massachusetts took its first step onto the world's largest sporting stage. And from that moment, volleyball would never look back.
