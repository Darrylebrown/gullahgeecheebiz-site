# The Basket That Built the Coast: Sweetgrass and the Gullah Geechee Economy

**By Darryl Elliott Brown | Gullah Geechee Biz | The Root — free edition**

---

Before there was a Lowcountry tourism industry, there was a Lowcountry economy.

It was carried in the hands of women who could coil a basket so tight it held rice, held water, held the memory of a home across the ocean. Sweetgrass basket weaving is the oldest known African American art form in the United States — and for three centuries it has been more than art. It has been livelihood, inheritance, identity, and, increasingly, the model for what a culture-first economy looks like.

This month we're taking that story seriously — because the basket that built the coast is also the blueprint for building something new.

---

## A Craft Carried Across the Atlantic

The roots of the sweetgrass basket reach back to West Africa, where coiled-grass basketry was practiced by the Mende and the Krio peoples of Sierra Leone and Liberia. When enslaved Africans were brought to the Sea Islands of South Carolina and Georgia, they brought that knowledge in their hands. On the rice plantations of the Lowcountry, basket making was not decoration — it was **infrastructure**. Baskets were used for winnowing rice, carrying produce, and storing everything a household needed. The skill was a survival skill.

Today the tradition lives on — most visibly on Charleston's Market Street and US-17's "Sweetgrass Basket Stands" before Mount Pleasant, where weavers still sit and coil in public, one basket at a time, each one taking days to complete.

## An Art Form at a Crossroads

But here is the honest tension in the story: the cultural center of this craft is under real pressure.

- **The grass itself is scarcer.** Sweetgrass (muhly grass) grows along the marsh edges, and development has eaten much of the open land where it thrived. Weavers now travel farther and pay more for the material — some substitute bulrush or pine straw, but traditionalists hold that a true basket is sweetgrass.
- **The weavers are aging.** Much of the craft's knowledge sits with elders, and there aren't enough young apprentices in the pipeline.
- **The market undervalues the maker.** A weekend basket that takes twenty hours to weave often sells for far less than its craft, labor, and history are worth.

None of this is new to those of us who've watched the cycle: when a culture is commodified without the community sharing in the value, the makers are the first to be squeezed out.

## Why This Is a Business Story

The "Gullah Geechee Biz" in our name isn't an accident. This newsletter is built on a conviction: **cultural preservation and economic empowerment are the same work.** The crispest proof is sitting on Market Street every day.

When you buy a sweetgrass basket directly from a weaver:

1. You keep a maker, not just a middleman, working.
2. You create an economic reason for the next generation to learn the craft — the only durable kind of preservation.
3. You fund the continuation of a three-hundred-year-old tradition with every single transaction.

This is the "culture economy" in miniature: heritage that actually pays its own way, so it survives beyond goodwill.

## What We're Doing About It

At Gullah Geechee Biz we're building the same principle into everything we publish. Our encyclopedia, our books, our documentaries aren't just records of the past — they're **revenue engines for the culture**, funding the translation work, the heirs'-property advocacy, and the artisans who carry the tradition forward.

And we're inviting you to participate in the sweetgrass economy the same way we all participate in a market — by **choosing where the money goes**.

Three ways to move the culture dollar:

1. **Buy from the makers.** Support Gullah Geechee artisans directly — sweetgrass basket weavers, quilters, and crafters are listed across the Corridor from the Carolinas to Florida.
2. **Learn a name.** The people who make the things carry the story. Know who coiled the basket before you gift it.
3. **Share the infrastructure.** When a weaver is old and wants to pass the skill on, what they need most is a market that will sustain an apprentice. That market starts with informed buyers.

Tend to the root, and the tree heals.
