## The Quietest Threat on the Coast

Heirs' property is the single greatest reason coastal Black families have lost land in the American South — and most people have never heard the phrase.

It works like this: a family owns land passed down informally. No will, no clear deed, sometimes no probate. When the original owner dies, ownership passes to *all* descendants, generation after generation. After two or three generations, a single acre might be co-owned by dozens of people scattered across several states. Nobody can sell it outright. Nobody can borrow against it. Nobody holds a clean title.

And that's exactly when the predators show up.

A developer or speculator acquires a tiny fractional interest from one distant heir for a few thousand dollars, then files a **partition action** in court to force a sale of the *entire* property. The family that has held that land for a hundred years watches it sold at a courthouse auction — often for far below market — and the land is gone. This is the quiet engine behind much of the displacement along the Gullah Geechee Corridor, from Hilton Head to Charleston to St. Helena Island.

The good news: it is preventable. It takes time, it takes documents, and it takes a family deciding to act *now* rather than later. Here is the five-step playbook we use and share with families in our network.

---

## STEP 1 — Write It Down, Decide Who It Belongs To

*(PAID)*

The first mistake families make is assuming that "everyone knows" who the land belongs to. In heirs' property, everyone knowing is not enough. The law needs names, dates, and a chain of title.

**Do this:**
- Assemble every scrap you have: old tax receipts, insurance policies, a copy of the deed, letters, photographs of the house with family writing on the back.
- Gather the family tree — names, birth and death dates of every ancestor who ever owned the land.
- Identify who currently lives on the property and who has been paying the taxes.

This exact list is what an attorney or legal aid clinic will ask for first. Having it ready turns a twelve-month case into a manageable one.

---

## STEP 2 — Get the Title Cleaned and Recorded

*(PAID)*

You cannot protect property you cannot prove you own. The single most powerful document you can create is a **quiet title action** — a court case that resolves ownership disputes and produces a clean, marketable title in the name of the living heirs.

**Do this:**
- Contact the county probate court in the county where the land sits.
- Ask about its **administrative order process** or **quiet title/barnacle** program. South Carolina, Georgia, and North Carolina all have processes designed to help heirs' property families — several are free of charge for qualifying low-income families.
- File, follow through, and get the final order *recorded* at the county register of deeds. An unrecorded order is as good as a rumor.

If the process sounds intimidating: legal aid clinics and land-loss prevention programs along the Corridor exist precisely to walk families through it, often at no cost.

---

## STEP 3 — Now Create a Family Entity

*(PAID)*

Once title is clean, the smartest move is to stop owning the land as a scattered group of individuals and own it together as one entity. This is the difference between land that can be picked off piece by piece and land that is structurally protected.

**The two strongest options:**

1. **A family limited liability company (LLC)** — every family member gets a percentage *of the company*, not a fractional deed to the land. One person, one vote. A fractional interest in an LLC is not the same as a deed interest, and it is dramatically harder for an outside buyer to force a partition sale.
2. **A land trust or a permanent conservation easement** — donate a development-rights restriction on the property. You keep living there and keep the land, but you legally make it impossible to build a subdivision on it. This permanently removes the highest-value bait and, in many cases, earns the family a federal tax benefit.

Both steps usually require a lawyer, but the entity-formation costs are modest compared to the value of the land they protect.

---

## STEP 4 — Never Sell a Fraction to a Stranger, Ever

*(PAID)*

Partition predators do not need most of the family — they need *one* fractional interest and a sympathetic judge. So the ironclad family rule is:

- **No family member may sell or borrow against their share without the whole family's agreement.**
- Put this in writing in the LLC operating agreement: a **right of first refusal** (the family gets to buy the share first) and a **buy-sell/prohibition clause** that keeps shares from going to outsiders.
- If a family member genuinely needs out, the family (or the entity) buys them out at a fair appraisal — privately, quietly, on your terms, never at a courthouse auction.

Every predatory partition case begins with one outsider holding one small piece of paper. Deny them the paper.

---

## STEP 5 — Get Representation on Retainer or in a Network

*(PAID)*

Do not face this alone, and do not wait until the partition notice is taped to the door. By then you are defending, not deciding.

**Do this:**
- Before you need a lawyer, find one. Keep a name, a number, and a file.
- Join the organizations doing this work along the Corridor — the land-loss prevention and heirs'-property programs in the Carolinas and Georgia, and the cultural organizations representing Sea Island families.
- The moment you hear "partition suit," call a land-use or real-property attorney and your State Bar low-income legal aid line **the same day**. Time is the predator's best friend; it is your enemy.

## One Family, One Acre, One Hundred Years

The land deficit the Black South carries is not an accident of history — it is a wound that has been reopened, acre by acre, by partition sales that were always preventable. The good news is that the tools to close the wound exist, they are legal, and in many places they are even free.

The hardest part is not the paperwork. It is a family deciding, together, that the land is worth the meeting, worth the money for an attorney, worth planning for a future that begins after the current elders are gone.

The land is not dirt. It is memory, it is identity, and — done right — it is a deed of belonging that no predator can ever pry loose.
